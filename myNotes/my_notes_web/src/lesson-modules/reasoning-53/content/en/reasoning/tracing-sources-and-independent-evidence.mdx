## Ten publications making the same statement

You encounter an astonishing claim. Initially skeptical, you search for confirmation and find ten articles on ten websites, all saying approximately the same thing. Different logos, authors and publication times make the agreement look impressive.

What have you discovered: ten pieces of evidence, or ten publications making the same statement?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="headline" />

The lake incident, publications and measurements in this lesson are **fictional teaching examples**, not a verified case study. Begin with the reports visible in the activity. Increase their number, then follow their citations one layer at a time.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-53" preset="publications" id="sources-publications" />

More visible reports do not yet establish more independent confirmations. But the possibility that reports share a source does not establish that their claim is false either. We need to discover what supports them.

## Follow the claim back to its origin

**Provenance** is the origin and transmission history of information. In the example, the publications cite a local newspaper or a news agency. Both rely on a press release, which in turn relies on one water-level measurement. Following the references backward makes apparently separate branches converge.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="provenance" />

Distinguish three things:

- A **claim** is the statement being evaluated: the lake level fell by 30 centimetres overnight.
- A **report** communicates that claim: an article, document or person’s testimony.
- **Evidence** consists of the observations, measurements or reasons supporting it.

Ten reports can communicate one claim based on one measurement. The original measurement might be excellent. Reading its repetition ten times has not supplied ten independently obtained measurements.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="mirror" />

Five reflections of a candle are not five candles. The metaphor concerns multiplication of support, not whether the original source is reliable. A copy can faithfully preserve valuable information while adding no new observation of the event.

These distinctions give us five separate questions:

| Concept | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Provenance | Where did the information originate and how did it travel? |
| Independence | What evidence or possible errors do the sources share? |
| Credibility | How reliable is this source for this particular claim? |
| Corroboration | Does additional evidence actually support the claim? |
| Detectability | Would we expect to find evidence if the claim were true? |

The aim is reasonable reliance on other people. We cannot personally investigate everything we know, and tracing a source is not a rule that only firsthand observation counts as knowledge.

## When additional evidence really matters

Now imagine three investigations. A municipal employee reads a manual gauge, a university team uses surveying equipment, and a monitoring station records a change using a separate sensor. They report changes of −30, −29 and −31 centimetres.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="convergence" />

The measurements are compatible with a roughly 30-centimetre fall in this illustration. Whether actual readings agree sufficiently would also depend on their uncertainty, time interval and reference level.

The important change is that the observations were obtained through separate routes. A fault in the first instrument would not, by itself, explain the other two. Agreement across genuinely separate investigations can therefore be harder to explain as one isolated error.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="comparison" />

However, different authors, organizations and even instruments do not guarantee independence from every possible error. Perhaps all three instruments used the same faulty calibration reference. Or perhaps the university team copied the municipal readings instead of making its own measurements.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-53" preset="independence" id="sources-independence" />

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="shared-error" />

With a shared calibration reference, there are still three measurement origins, but a possible common failure mechanism. If one team merely copies another, there are fewer separately obtained observations. **Counting distinct origins and establishing statistical independence are different tasks.** Independence should be assessed relative to the relevant uncertainties, assumptions and error mechanisms; a citation diagram alone cannot settle all of them.

### The probability connection

For events $A$ and $B$, the general multiplication rule, when $P(A)>0$, is

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).
$$

If the events are independent, then

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).
$$

Suppose $A$ means that the first article reports the claim and $B$ means that a second article repeats it. Once the first report is known, a faithful copy may be expected whether the underlying claim is true or false. It may add little or no evidence about the original event.

When combining reports as evidence for a hypothesis $H$, the relevant independence assumption is often **conditional independence under each competing hypothesis**, not merely independence of publication names. We must ask what the second report adds after the first is already known. A report that also conducts a new check is different from a pure copy.

### Expert consensus and repetition

A consensus supported by numerous experiments, diverse methods, criticism and attempts at replication differs from a hundred people repeating an unsupported assertion. Yet expert opinions need not be statistically independent: experts share evidence, methods and training. The strength of a consensus depends on the quality and diversity of its evidential basis and the processes used to examine conclusions.

The question is how the agreement was produced, rather than simply how many people agree.

## Credibility concerns a source, a claim and a context

We cannot personally measure every lake, inspect every bridge, reproduce every experiment or witness every historical event. Much of our knowledge comes through **testimony**: what others tell us or communicate in writing. Philosophers disagree about exactly when testimony justifies belief; its importance does not depend on treating every assertion as equally reliable.

Instead of asking whether a person is generally trustworthy, examine four dimensions for this claim.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="credibility" />

The fictional hydrologist understands measurement but has not examined this lake. The fisherman was present but lacks specialist measurement skills. The company representative has data access and a possible incentive to minimize a problem. The technician has relevant training, access and a documented record of accurate measurements.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-53" preset="credibility" id="sources-credibility" />

These descriptions do not establish a universal hierarchy. The fisherman might be well placed to report a strange noise yesterday; the hydrologist might be better placed to explain possible physical mechanisms. Neither necessarily knows the precise size or cause of this particular change.

Expertise without access can be insufficient. Access without expertise can lead to misinterpretation. Both can coexist with incentives worth examining. Indicators left unspecified remain unknown, rather than automatically reassuring. There is no justified numerical credibility score such as 85% in these brief profiles.

## A conflict of interest is a reason for scrutiny

Suppose a company extracting water from the lake publishes a report saying its activities did not affect the level. It has a financial interest in that conclusion. What follows?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="conflict" />

A person can have an incentive to defend a conclusion that happens to be true. Someone with excellent intentions can reach a false conclusion. A conflict is relevant to investigating reliability, but it does not logically refute the claim.

Examine how the evidence was selected, whether the method and data are transparent, and whether others can verify the result.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="incentives" />

The possible influence acts on the process of selection and interpretation. It does not directly determine the truth value of the conclusion. Establishing that distortion actually occurred requires further evidence.

<Exercise question="A company funds a report favorable to its activities. What should you investigate before accepting or rejecting the report?">
Inspect the actual claim, data access, measurement and analysis methods, selective omissions, and the possibility of verification by others. Funding can make additional scrutiny warranted. It does not by itself show that the result is false; ignoring a relevant conflict would also leave a reliability question unanswered.
</Exercise>

## When is absence of evidence informative?

Someone says a large red ball is somewhere in a room. You look inside and find nothing. Does that observation count against the claim?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="rooms" />

A ball would be difficult to miss in a small, empty, well-lit room. It might remain unnoticed in a large, dark room full of boxes. The observation “no ball found” is the same, but the search procedures predict it differently if a ball exists.

Let $H$ mean that the ball is present, and $N$ mean no detection. The chance of missing an existing ball is

$$
P(N\mid H).
$$

The smaller this probability, the more surprising a failed search is under $H$. Adjust the hypothetical detection chance and examine the corresponding missed searches. Every dot represents a possible search **conditional on the ball being present**, not a person’s credibility or an inferred probability of absence.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-53" preset="search" id="sources-search" />

If the chance of detecting a present ball is 90%, then

$$
P(N\mid H)=1-0.9=0.1.
$$

But to decide whether no detection favors absence over presence, compare $P(N\mid H)$ with $P(N\mid\neg H)$. A failed search counts against $H$ when it is less expected under $H$ than under the alternative, provided the observed event has positive probability.

Crucially, a conditional probability in one direction is not generally equal to the conditional probability in the other direction:

$$
P(N\mid H)\not\equiv P(H\mid N).
$$

A 10% chance of missing an existing ball does not automatically mean a 10% chance that a ball exists after the search. The notation above means there is no general identity; particular numerical values can coincide.

### An explicit Bayesian extension

The optional panel adds assumptions beyond the original search illustration: no detection is certain when the ball is absent, so $P(N\mid\neg H)=1$, and a prior $p=P(H)$ is chosen. Write $s$ for the detection chance given presence. For $1-ps>0$,

$$
P(H\mid N)=\frac{p(1-s)}{p(1-s)+(1-p)}=\frac{p(1-s)}{1-ps}.
$$

With $p=0.5$ and $s=0.9$, the result is $1/11\approx9.1\%$, not 10%. Changing the prior changes the result. At $p=s=1$, no detection is impossible under this model, so conditioning on it is undefined. If a procedure could report a detection even when the ball was absent, its alternative-hypothesis likelihood would also need to be specified.

The same reasoning applies to historical records, literature searches, equipment diagnostics and attempts to locate independent confirmation. Ask: **If this claim were true, how likely would this search be to find the relevant evidence?** Also ask how likely the observed result is under competing explanations. A slogan about absence of evidence cannot replace those questions.

## Reconstruct the evidence behind seven reports

Return to the lake. This time, distinguish the seven communications from the investigations behind them. The journalist’s organizational label does not imply that the journalist obtained new measurements.

| Report | What it relies on |
| --- | --- |
| A — Local newspaper | Municipal measurement |
| B — News website | Article A |
| C — Social media account | Article B |
| D — University report | Its own field measurements |
| E — News magazine | Article D |
| F — Independent journalist | Articles A and D |
| G — Monitoring station | Its own sensor readings |

How many distinct evidential origins are represented? After answering, trace individual reports. Then consider what would be needed to establish independence.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-53" preset="workshop" id="sources-workshop" />

<Exercise question="Explain which reports share origins. Can the citation network establish that all remaining origins have independent errors?">
There are three distinct measurement origins: the municipal measurement M, university field measurements U, and monitoring-station readings S. A, B and C share M; D and E share U. F combines reports about M and U without adding a new measurement. G supplies the route to S.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="workshop" />

That count does not establish independence. Investigate instruments, calibration, sampling, assumptions, processing and other shared sources of error. The network tells us the stated provenance, not whether every dependency has been disclosed or the measurements are accurate.
</Exercise>

## A source-tracing method

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="method" />

Following citations to their original context and reading laterally about sources can help you apply this method. Do not replace counting publications with the equally simple rule of counting supposedly independent sources: their quality still matters.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-53" figure="synthesis" />

Most of what we know comes from other people. This allows knowledge to grow beyond any individual’s experience. Information can also be copied, reformulated and republished a thousand times without gaining new support.

Ask how many different reasons support the claim, how independent they are, and how good those reasons are. **Follow the evidence, not merely the repetition.**
