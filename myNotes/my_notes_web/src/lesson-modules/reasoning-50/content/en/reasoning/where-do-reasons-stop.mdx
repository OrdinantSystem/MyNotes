If every belief needs a reason, and every reason needs another reason, how can we ever be justified in believing anything?

Evaluating an argument asks whether its conclusion follows from its premises. A deeper question asks what supports those premises. The distinction between being right and having good reasons for being right does not disappear when we move back one step.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="chain" />

Four questions guide the investigation:

1. What justifies a belief?
2. What justifies the reasons supporting it?
3. Must justification eventually terminate somewhere?
4. Can justification arise from relationships among beliefs?

These lead to the **regress problem of justification**, a central problem in epistemology.

## How do you know it rained?

Someone wakes up, looks through a window and notices a wet street. They form the belief that it rained last night.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="rain" />

Why believe that? Because the street is wet. Why believe the street is wet? Because they can see it. Why should seeing it provide a reason to believe it is wet? Because perception is generally reliable under ordinary conditions. And what supports that claim?

A familiar inference has exposed assumptions that were initially invisible. Follow those dependencies, then compare two other views of the same rain belief.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-50" preset="architecture" id="aor50-architecture" />

Asking “why?” moves toward a supporting reason. In the diagram, support arrows point in the opposite direction: from the reason toward the belief it supports. The rain belief retains its identity across all three views.

A wet street provides possible evidence of rain, not a deductive proof. Street cleaning or a sprinkler could also explain the observation. “Rain can make streets wet” does not license the converse inference as a certainty.

Distinguish **what caused a belief** from **what makes the belief reasonable**. Fear, habit and wishful thinking can cause someone to believe a proposition without justifying it. This lesson concerns the second question.

## The regress problem

Suppose we believe $B_0$ and support it by another belief, $B_1$. If $B_1$ also requires justification, we may appeal to $B_2$, and then to $B_3$.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="regress" />

The arrow means **provides support for**. It need not express logical implication. If every justified belief must receive its justification from another justified belief, the demand appears to continue:

$$
\cdots\longrightarrow B_4\longrightarrow B_3\longrightarrow B_2\longrightarrow B_1\longrightarrow B_0.
$$

Where does the justification come from?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="structures" />

An infinite chain raises a question about how the required reasons can be available without completing an endless task. A circular chain risks assuming what it should establish. A terminating chain requires an account of why its starting points are justified.

This is a philosophical problem, not a mathematical proof that knowledge is impossible. **Infinitism** defends an infinite regress of reasons; its defenders need not claim that a person literally recites infinitely many reasons at once. The lesson concentrates on two other influential responses: foundationalism and coherentism.

## Foundationalism: justification without another inference

Return to the wet street and separate three propositions:

1. It rained last night.
2. The street is wet.
3. I am having a visual experience of what appears to be a wet street.

The first concerns an inferred past event. The second concerns the external world. The third concerns present experience. Even if the street is not wet, the appearance of wetness could still be genuinely experienced.

Could some beliefs receive justification directly from experience rather than by inference from another belief? This motivates **foundationalism**: some beliefs have basic justification, and other justified beliefs ultimately depend on them.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="foundation" />

**A basic belief is not necessarily a belief without justification. Its justification does not depend on another belief.** Nor does this mean that the belief justifies itself in a circle. A foundationalist may appeal to experience as a non-inferential source of support.

Foundationalism is not permission to choose arbitrary assertions as foundations. Theories disagree about which beliefs can be basic and how strong their support must be. Some allow basic justification to be fallible. A candidate foundation must still be defended; a line drawn beneath it does not establish its status. See the [Stanford Encyclopedia’s account of foundationalist justification](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justep-foundational/).

### Appearance and reality

The Müller-Lyer figure distinguishes the apparent length of a line from its measured length. Compare the horizontal shafts, excluding their angled wings.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-50" preset="illusion" id="aor50-illusion" />

The shafts have the same horizontal extent and equal lengths. The comparison is also available without operating the control: each shaft spans 390 drawing units. Viewers need not experience the illusion equally strongly.

A belief that one shaft is longer can be mistaken, while “it appears longer to me” may accurately report the experience. This does not establish that all reports of experience are infallible. Even if experience provides immediate justification, how far does that support extend to claims about the external world? The foundationalist still has work to do.

## Coherentism: beliefs within a wider system

Instead of tracing one chain downward, expand the rain example. The street and rooftops are wet. A neighbour remembers hearing rain. A local weather station recorded precipitation. The soil is damp.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="network" />

These fallible observations and reports fit a common explanation. **Coherentism** locates justification in the way a belief fits within a wider system of beliefs. It concerns more than the absence of contradictions: explanatory and evidential relationships matter.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="consistency" />

“The street is wet,” “seven is prime” and “elephants are mammals” can all be true without providing meaningful support for one another. Consistency is therefore weaker than coherence.

The rain network illustrates how mutually reinforcing evidence can matter. It does not prove that coherentism is the correct theory. Foundationalists can also value coherent evidence. The deeper disagreement concerns whether coherence can generate justification or must ultimately depend on a more basic source.

Coherentism about **justification** should also be distinguished from a coherence theory of **truth**. A view about what justifies accepting a proposition need not claim that fitting a system makes the proposition true. The [Stanford Encyclopedia’s coherentism article](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justep-coherence/) develops this distinction and the objections below.

## Coherence, circularity and source dependence

Consider a bare circular argument: “A is true because B is true; B is true because A is true.”

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="circle" />

Without additional support, this exchanges the burden of justification rather than meeting it. A wider network can contain different observations, explanatory relations, alternative explanations and separately checkable reports. Those features distinguish it from the bare loop. They do not guarantee that every network escapes circularity; circularity remains a real challenge for coherentist theories.

### Five websites, one report

Suppose five websites all report that it rained. Do they provide five independent confirmations? Investigate the reporting relationships.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-50" preset="sources" id="aor50-sources" />

In this hypothetical example all five copied one original report. Five publications therefore have one reporting origin. Repetition does not add five independent sources. The original report might still be accurate and well supported; dependence alone does not show that it is false.

A network’s strength depends on what the connections represent, whether sources are dependent and how the system responds to conflicting evidence. Counting nodes or connections cannot settle those questions.

### The isolation objection

Imagine a detective’s elaborate crime theory. Every part fits together beautifully, but the original evidence has been misunderstood.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="isolation" />

The theory could be internally coherent while failing to describe reality. The **isolation objection** asks how internal agreement connects beliefs with the world outside the system. It is not answered merely by drawing more links.

## Different starting points, different standards

Definitions, axioms, premises, observations and assumptions all occur in reasoning, but their roles differ. A definition fixes a use of a term; it does not by itself establish an empirical claim. A premise may be temporarily granted in an argument without being an axiom or a justified belief about the world.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="starting-points" />

A formal axiom is evaluated within a specified mathematical system. An empirical assumption may require testing, calibration or sensitivity analysis. An everyday default, such as trusting ordinary perception, can be reasonable without a fresh investigation each time while remaining open to revision when there is a reason for doubt.

### Changing the geometrical starting point

Given a line and a point outside it, Euclidean geometry has exactly one parallel line through that point. Other geometries have different consequences.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="geometry" />

On the sphere, where “lines” are great circles, distinct great circles meet at antipodal points. Their planes pass through the sphere’s centre and intersect in a line through that centre, giving those two intersection points. There are no parallel great circles. This is a comparison of geometries, not a claim that spherical geometry preserves every Euclidean axiom apart from the parallel postulate.

In the hyperbolic disk model, the boundary is not part of the plane. Lines are diameters or arcs of circles orthogonal to that boundary. Infinitely many lines through the marked point do not meet the horizontal baseline inside the disk. Here “parallel” means nonintersecting; this includes more than the two limiting parallels sometimes singled out in hyperbolic terminology.

Different axioms and interpretations can give different consequences without making mathematics contradictory. Write

$$
\Gamma\vdash T.
$$

Here $\Gamma$ is a collection of premises or axioms, $T$ is a theorem, and $\vdash$ means formal derivability using the specified rules. The proof establishes derivability within that system. Whether the system appropriately models physical space is a further empirical question.

## Dissect the support for a belief

Consider this hypothetical belief: **Retrieval practice helps me remember information better than rereading.** The task examines the roles of supporting statements; it does not establish a learning effect for you or for everyone.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-50" preset="classification" id="aor50-classification" />

<Exercise question="What does each proposed reason contribute, and what would you check next?">

**Reference discussion, with an added qualification.** “I remembered more after testing myself” describes an outcome: observation in the simplified example, though the report may depend on memory and measurement. It does not identify a cause.

“Testing myself caused the improvement” is a causal inference. Differences in prior knowledge, material difficulty, learning time or assessment might offer alternative explanations.

“A researcher reported similar findings” is testimony for someone relying on that report. Its value depends on the original study, its methods, its source and its relevance here.

“Both methods were given equal study time” is treated in this activity as a methodological assumption for a fair comparison. If timing records establish it, the same sentence can instead report an observation. These categories describe evidential roles in context, not permanent properties of sentences. Equal time also does not by itself establish that every relevant condition was comparable.

## Justification without absolute certainty

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="questions" />

We began with an apparently simple belief and uncovered observations, experiences, inferences, memories and assumptions. The structures offer different ways to think about that support.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-50" figure="synthesis" />

Difficulty obtaining absolute certainty does not make all beliefs equally reasonable. Careful observation, reliable sources and sound inference provide a different epistemic position from wishful thinking alone.

The ultimate source and structure of justification remain contested. We need not settle every issue in epistemology before improving our reasoning. We can make dependencies explicit, look for defeaters and revise a conclusion when its support changes.

**What supports what you believe—and what does that support itself depend on?**
