A premise can be completely true, emotionally compelling and offered by an intelligent, trustworthy person—and still fail to establish a conclusion. Evaluating an argument requires three different questions: Is the premise true? Is it relevant to this conclusion? Does it provide enough support?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="dimensions" />

Mapping an argument identifies the inferential connections. We now ask whether those connections deserve to be there. The skill is to locate why a reason succeeds or fails, not simply to attach an impressive name to a mistake.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="bridge" />

The bridge represents a relationship between what we have established and what we want to establish. A missing assumption can leave it incomplete. Sometimes a premise supports something meaningful, but a different conclusion from the one being claimed.

## 1. The same expert, two different questions

Imagine a fictional structural engineer with twenty years of experience inspecting bridges. They examine the calculations, materials and current inspection reports for a particular bridge, then judge it safe under its specified operating conditions. That assessment carries evidential weight.

Keep the person exactly the same: education, intelligence, experience, confidence and reputation. Now ask whether a company’s stock will increase next month. Suppose they predict that it will, without financial expertise or supporting evidence. Should the same credentials carry the same weight?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="expert" />

Nothing about the person changed. The relationship between their expertise and the conclusion changed. Are we evaluating how impressive a reason sounds, or what it actually permits us to conclude?

The examples throughout this lesson are fictional. The connections and indicators are qualitative teaching devices, not probability estimates or measured psychological data.

## 2. Why logical form alone is not enough

Consider the formal pattern of affirming the consequent. Its defect can be recognized without knowing what $P$ and $Q$ mean. The premises can both hold while the conclusion fails.

Now consider: “An expert believes $P$. Therefore, $P$ is probably true.” Its strength depends on who the expert is, what their expertise covers, what $P$ says and how they reached their judgment.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="formal" />

This motivates **informal fallacies**: defects that often become clear through meaning, background assumptions, evidence and the circumstances of the reasoning. “Informal” does not mean that the standards are casual. It means that evaluating the argument requires more than its abstract form.

### Relevance is a relationship

Relevance is not an intrinsic property of a statement. Keep this premise fixed: “It is raining heavily outside.” Change only the conclusion.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-52" preset="relevance" id="aor52-relevance" />

The rain can support wet roads, matter without deciding a concert cancellation, and establish nothing about primality. A fact need not become false when it stops supporting the particular conclusion under discussion.

The **acceptability, relevance and sufficiency (ARS)** framework separates three obligations. The premises must be adequately supported; they must bear on the conclusion; and the overall support must justify the proposed confidence. Relevance alone does not establish sufficiency.

## 3. When should we trust an expert?

Return to the engineer. The compressed argument is: “The expert says $C$; therefore $C$ is probably true.” Several background conditions explain why this could be reasonable.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="warrants" />

Expert testimony is a legitimate source of evidence. We cannot personally repeat every experiment, engineering calculation or historical investigation on which our knowledge depends. The rational alternative to blind trust is to evaluate the conditions under which testimony deserves weight, rather than blindly reject it.

Compare the original assessment with variations in which a supporting condition is absent or undermined. Each case concerns the same safety claim; the lines name conditions, not numerical degrees of truth.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-52" preset="authority" id="aor52-authority" />

An **appeal to inappropriate authority**, traditionally *argumentum ad verecundiam*, assigns weight to an authority whose relevant competence or reliability has not been established—or gives it more weight than it deserves. Consulting an authority is not itself a fallacy.

An inference is **defeasible** when it can reasonably be accepted on the available evidence but may need to be withdrawn when new information appears. Learning that the engineer relied on an outdated inspection report can undermine an earlier reasonable assessment. The earlier acceptance was not necessarily irrational; the evidence available to us changed.

A testimony-based bridge can strengthen or weaken. It does not behave like an irreversible mathematical proof. Even relevant, well-grounded testimony remains fallible.

## 4. When do emotions become evidence?

A fictional town is considering whether to demolish its community library. Two arguments arise.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="library" />

Both may produce sadness, but their relationships to their conclusions differ. Losing access to a study space or a valued meeting place gives us a reason to reconsider a decision. Sadness about an unpleasant fact does not make that fact false.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="emotion-roles" />

Separate “What is true?” from “What should we do?” Facts about human well-being can matter to both kinds of question, but must actually bear on the particular claim. The practical argument gives reasons to consider preservation; it does not settle costs, safety, alternatives or the final decision by itself.

For the fixed factual claim that the building is structurally sound, vary the emotional presentation and the presence of relevant technical evidence independently.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-52" preset="persuasion" id="aor52-persuasion" />

A **fallacious appeal to emotion** occurs when emotional persuasion replaces or distracts from the evidence needed for a conclusion. An argument can become more moving without gaining evidential support. But making us feel something does not itself discredit an argument. Ask whether the feeling corresponds to a relevant consideration.

## 5. Criticizing the person or the argument?

A fictional researcher reports: “Our study indicates that students retain information better when they distribute learning over several days.”

Response A says the groups studied for different total amounts of time, so the experiment cannot isolate the effect of distributing learning. Response B calls the researcher arrogant and unpleasant, then declares the conclusion false. Both criticize something; only the first directly challenges the study’s inferential support.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="criticism" />

Keep the researcher, reported evidence, inferential connection and conclusion separate. An attack on the researcher’s personality leaves the evidence-to-conclusion relationship unexamined. A methodological objection targets that relationship.

### Personal information can be relevant

Suppose the researcher fabricated measurements. This concerns the person but directly affects the evidence’s reliability. A financial conflict can justify additional scrutiny, although it does not independently show that the conclusion is false.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-52" preset="criticism" id="aor52-criticism" />

| Criticism | What it establishes |
| --- | --- |
| The researcher is unpleasant. | Little or nothing about the study’s validity. |
| The researcher has a financial conflict. | A potential reason for additional scrutiny. |
| The researcher fabricated the measurements. | A direct reason to distrust the affected evidence. |

An **ad hominem** problem arises when information about a person is used to dismiss a claim without a warranted connection to its truth or justification. Mentioning character or circumstances is not automatically fallacious. Calling every criticism of a source “ad hominem” would replace reasoning with another kind of labeling.

## 6. Find the missing connection

Use four questions whenever someone offers a reason.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="method" />

Consider an advertisement: “A famous athlete uses these headphones. Therefore, they have superior sound quality.” The premise establishes product use; the conclusion concerns sound quality. What would connect them?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="headphones" />

The athlete might choose the product for sponsorship, comfort, appearance, convenience or sound. The premise does not distinguish these explanations, so the proposed assumption has not been adequately supported. This is a reconstruction to examine, not a license to attribute an exact hidden thought to the speaker. Consider other plausible interpretations too.

Try the following arguments before opening the reference reasoning. Identify the conclusion, what the reason actually establishes, a possible connecting assumption and whether that assumption is warranted. A relevant reason may still need additional support.

## 7. A bad argument can reach a true conclusion

“Every prime is odd. Therefore, 17 is prime because it is odd.” The conclusion is true, but the reasoning is defective. The general premise is false too: 2 is prime and even.

Even repairing the premise to “Every prime greater than 2 is odd” does not validate the converse. An odd number need not be prime.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="fallacy" />

This is the **fallacy fallacy**: treating the failure of an argument for $P$ as grounds to infer that $P$ is false. A defective argument gives us a reason to reject or reduce confidence in that argument, not automatically to reject its conclusion.

For independent justification that 17 is prime, any composite integer has a prime divisor no greater than its square root. Since $\sqrt{17}<5$, only 2 and 3 need checking, and neither divides 17. This added check separates the truth of the conclusion from the failed oddness argument.

We can reject someone’s reasoning while agreeing with the conclusion for other reasons. Being correct and having good reasons for being correct are different achievements.

## 8. Relevance before persuasion

Return to the bridge. For the particular factual claim under evaluation, keep only the reasons that actually bear on it.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-52" figure="closing" />

The quality of a reason depends on what it permits us to conclude. Prestige, confidence or emotional force cannot substitute for a missing connection. Nor can a fallacy label substitute for explaining the defect.

The skill is not naming the mistake. It is locating it—and showing why the connection succeeds or fails.
