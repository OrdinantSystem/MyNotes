Knowing how reasoning should work does not ensure that we remember to apply it. External procedures can make thinking observable, testable and correctable. Preserve reasons before the outcome becomes known, then compare them with evidence. The aim is not to eliminate mistakes, but to make them informative.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="loop" />

## 1. Right, or lucky?

Two people independently organize a community workshop. A examines attendance records, asks about availability and compares disadvantages. B chooses Saturday because it is a favorite day. Both choose Saturday, and each attracts 32 attendees. Same decision, same outcome, different reasoning.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="opening" />

Have they demonstrated the same decision-making ability? Changing circumstances could expose differences between their approaches. If unexpected transport disruption reduces A’s attendance, was A’s earlier reasoning suddenly worse? Distinguish “Did it work?” from “Was it justified given what could reasonably have been known?”

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="quadrants" />

This conceptual grid is not a numerical quality scale. Rewarded weak reasoning can produce confidence without competence; an unfortunate outcome can lead someone to abandon a reasonable method. **Outcome bias** evaluates a decision differently because its outcome is known. Baron and Hershey’s experiments concern this distinction, not proof that this lesson’s template eliminates bias.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="probabilities" />

$$
P(\text{success}\mid A)=0.8,\qquad P(\text{success}\mid B)=0.2.
$$

With reliable probabilities and equivalent costs and consequences, A is preferable. Yet A can fail and B can succeed. One result does not reveal the entire generating distribution. Outcomes still matter: they can improve estimates and reveal neglected factors. Learn from results without letting a result alone define the quality of earlier reasoning.

## 2. Reconstructing the past

Suppose we expect approximately 30 attendees and only 12 arrive. Days later, we may sincerely remember being more pessimistic or having emphasized a risk we barely considered. This introductory example is separate from the detailed record below, whose central forecast is 28.

**Hindsight bias** makes earlier uncertainty difficult to reconstruct after we know the result. What confidence did we have? Which alternatives did we consider? A remembered story is not a reliable substitute for a contemporaneous record.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="hindsight" />

Create a snapshot while uncertainty still exists. Keep the original number fixed; add observations and revisions rather than silently rewriting it.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="snapshot" />

## 3. Build the record before the result

A fictional community organization wants a free introductory mathematics workshop. It has two available dates and an online option. Attendance estimates are predictions, not future facts.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="options" />

Online looks attractive on attendance and cost, but the organizers also value meaningful interaction and learning in person. A decision depends on predictions **and priorities**. They choose Saturday within a €120 budget, recording the following nine parts before the event.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="record" />

Each part addresses a reasoning problem. Alternatives preserve real choices. Evidence records what was available then. Assumptions expose uncertain dependencies. Forecasts permit later comparison. Revision conditions identify reasons to reconsider before we become attached to the outcome. The plausible 20–35 range is not a formally calibrated confidence interval.

Only after understanding the concrete record, compress its structure:

$$
R_t=(G,A,E_t,H,F,D,C).
$$

Here $G$ means goals and priorities; $A$ alternatives; $E_t$ evidence available at time $t$; $H$ assumptions and hypotheses; $F$ forecasts; $D$ the selected decision; and $C$ revision conditions. The subscript matters: tomorrow’s information may differ from today’s. The notation is a summary, not a requirement to mathematize ordinary planning.

## 4. Turn principles into procedures

A group member says “about 40” before everyone else estimates attendance. Other judgments may anchor on that number despite different information. Record individual estimates **before discussion**, then compare their reasons.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="estimates" />

The mean of the hypothetical independent estimates is $(40+21+26)/3=29$. Independence reduces one source of dependence; it guarantees neither accuracy nor freedom from shared mistaken assumptions. Discussion can still be useful after the initial estimates are preserved.

Record at least one serious alternative when one exists. This does not require every imaginable option: it prevents confusing the first solution with the only reasonable one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="alternatives" />

Checks should be proportionate. Lunch, housing and a long project do not deserve identical investigation. Ask whether information is likely to improve the decision enough to justify its cost. Consequences, reversibility and urgency matter; an exhausting procedure for trivial choices will not be sustainable.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="effort" />

The record says registrations below 15 at the deadline **or** announced major disruption trigger reconsideration. Exactly 15 does not satisfy the first condition. Reconsidering does not automatically mean cancellation. If a condition changes, preserve its earlier version and explain the change.

This applies counterexamples and testing rather than protecting beliefs: identify relevant contrary evidence before encountering it.

## 5. Separate observation from evaluation

There are 24 registrations, so the registration trigger is not met. On the event morning, unexpected transport disruption occurs. Only 12 attend; 10 pass the short comprehension task; the cost is €100. The fixed record predicted 28 and set three separate success criteria.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="observations" />

$$
e=y-\hat y=12-28=-16,\qquad \frac{10}{12}\times100\%\approx83.3\%.
$$

Attendance was 16 below the central forecast and eight below the minimum success criterion. Comprehension among attendees met the target, and the budget was respected. This mixed outcome cannot be compressed into one unqualified success/failure label. Change observations below while the original forecast and criteria stay fixed. The zero-attendance case has no defined comprehension percentage.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-59" preset="review" id="correction-review" />

Now evaluate **how the decision was made**. Were alternatives examined? Were forecasts grounded? What transport problems were foreseeable, and was a practical contingency available? Distinguish an immediate external event from a potentially correctable process weakness.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="diagnosis" />

Unexpected disruption does not automatically show negligent reasoning. Dependence on one transport route might nevertheless justify a proportionate contingency next time. Nor can we conclude that online would have succeeded: that alternative was not conducted and its outcome is unknown.

Create version 2 beside version 1. The 10-of-12 task result does not establish that in-person interaction caused learning. Retain that claim as a working assumption while seeking comparative evidence. A single cost observation supports a similar-event estimate only provisionally.

## 6. Learn across records

The supplied fictional graph depicts eight forecasts and outcomes. The values below reproduce its points and reported summaries. The first four consistently overestimate attendance; the last four have smaller errors. Keeping separate records can make a recurring pattern visible that isolated stories could conceal.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="history" />

$$
\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n|y_i-\hat y_i|,\qquad
\operatorname{ME}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i).
$$

The first four have MAE $10$ and ME $-10$; the last four have MAE $1.75$ and ME $0.25$. Absolute error hides direction; signed errors reveal habitual overestimation or underestimation, but can cancel. Neither statistic alone explains the cause of a change. The fictional series does not establish that decision records caused improvement.

Kahneman and Klein discuss how intuitive expertise depends on learnable environmental regularities and sufficiently informative feedback. Repetition alone does not guarantee improvement. Feedback itself may be noisy, incomplete or misleading.

## 7. Your first record

Choose a modest decision you expect to make soon. Complete what is useful **before** learning its outcome. Be specific without false precision. Prepare a visible export, then copy it somewhere you control if you want to return later. This page does not automatically save it or schedule a reminder.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-59" preset="journal" id="correction-journal" />

For a study-method example, compare one hour of passive rereading with one hour of practice problems. Predict a measurable change on comparable exercises and review after two weeks, accounting for topic difficulty, prior knowledge and time spent studying. This proposed comparison does not perfectly establish causation; it makes reasons and outcomes more inspectable.

## 8. Design a reasoning environment

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-59" figure="levels" />

Reason about the world, examine your reasons, and design procedures that support future reasoning. Records connect counterexamples, revision conditions, probability, independent evidence, disagreement, intuition, facts versus values and decision trade-offs. The document is only an instrument; the subject is a process that can examine and modify itself.

Each review should inform the next decision. Some assumptions survive, others change. Progress does not mean never being wrong: it means recognizing errors, understanding what the evidence warrants, and allowing that learning to change what happens next.

## Sources and limits

The decision-record template is an original pedagogical synthesis, not a universally validated intervention that reliably eliminates bias. The workshop, thresholds and dataset are fictional. A prediction and outcome alone do not identify a causal explanation.
