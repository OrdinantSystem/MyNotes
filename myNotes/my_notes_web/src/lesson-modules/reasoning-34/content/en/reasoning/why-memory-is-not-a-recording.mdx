## 2. The central idea

We tend to imagine memory as a recording device. Something happens, the brain stores it, and when we want to remember it, we play it back.

But remembering is not simply replaying the past. It involves reconstructing an experience using retained information, existing knowledge, expectations and, potentially, information acquired afterward.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="models" />

The important conclusion is not that memory is unreliable and should be discarded. Rather, remembering something, being convinced that it happened, and establishing that it actually happened are three different things.

**Distinguish the content of a memory from its origin and its accuracy.** We will use the room to separate encoding, retention and retrieval; examine reconstruction and later suggestions; and learn to investigate a memory without automatically accepting or dismissing it.

## 3. Three opportunities for memory to fail

Memory is not a single operation. There are several processes between experiencing something and reporting it later. [OpenStax, How Memory Functions](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/8-1-how-memory-functions) introduces encoding, storage and retrieval; here, retention names the continued availability of information over time.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="processes" />

### Encoding: what entered your memory?

Suppose you paid attention to the person's movement and the blue bag but barely noticed the clock.

The clock was present in the original scene. Nevertheless, its exact time might not have been successfully encoded. A detail can be present in our environment without becoming something we can subsequently remember. This continues the distinction between an event and our observation of it.

Imagine attention as a soft spotlight: some objects remain recognizable while other details become faint. This is a metaphor for selective encoding, not a claim that the brain stores a literal picture.

### Retention: what remains over time?

Even if you initially noticed that the clock showed 8:20, the detail might become difficult to recall later.

Other information can interfere with what we remember, and the accessibility of information can change over time.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="retention" />

Failing to retrieve a detail does not necessarily establish that the information has been permanently lost. The illustration shows one possible change in accessibility, not a universal forgetting curve.

### Retrieval: what do you reconstruct?

Now imagine someone asks what time the clock showed. You remember that it was approximately eight o'clock. You might reconstruct the answer as 8:15, even though the original time was 8:20.

The resulting recollection can contain accurate elements alongside inaccurate ones.

| Original event | Possible recollection |
| --- | --- |
| Blue bag | Blue bag |
| Red cup | Red cup |
| Green book | Uncertain |
| 8:20 | 8:15 |

The key distinction is between a missing detail and an incorrectly reconstructed detail. In the first case, we do not know. In the second, we may mistakenly believe that we know.

## 4. Building a picture from incomplete information

Instead of presenting memory as a collection of photographs stored somewhere in the brain, represent remembering as the construction of a picture using information of different origins.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="construction" />

The brain does not necessarily preserve every component of an experience with equal precision. Existing knowledge helps us interpret and reconstruct events, which is often useful but can also introduce inaccuracies.

Suppose you remember visiting a restaurant several years ago. You remember sitting near a window, talking with a friend and eating something. When imagining the restaurant now, you might also picture menus, cutlery and glasses.

Some of these details may come from the actual experience. Others may come from your general knowledge of what restaurants are like. The difficulty is that these components do not necessarily feel different when they appear together in a recollection.

Imagine individual pieces assembling into one coherent image. Their origin labels disappear, and the scene looks completely natural. Reverse the construction and restore the labels: the pieces need not have come from the same source.

**A coherent recollection is not necessarily an accurate recollection.** Completeness, vividness and coherence are not guarantees of historical accuracy. The [source-monitoring framework](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346328/) examines how we attribute information to its origins.

## 5. How a question can change an answer

Return to the room. Consider another question:

> What color was the umbrella that the person brought into the room?

Pause before reading on. You might immediately recognize a problem, hesitate, try to imagine an umbrella or construct a possible answer.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="questions" />

The second question asks about the color of an object before establishing whether the object existed in the scene at all. The wording changes the problem being investigated, connecting this example with the course's treatment of language and meaning.

### Loftus and Palmer's original research

In their 1974 experiments, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed participants films of traffic accidents and varied the language used in subsequent questions. Different verbs describing the collision produced different estimates of the cars' speed.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="study" />

These are group means from Experiment 1, not a trajectory of one person's memory. In the original order, the means were 40.8 mph for *smashed*, 39.3 for *collided*, 38.1 for *bumped*, 34.0 for *hit* and 31.8 for *contacted*. The chart rounds them to whole mph. [Loftus & Palmer, original paper](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/c/571/files/2024/08/LoftusPalmer_ReconstructionAuto_JVLVB1974.pdf)

In a separate experiment, participants who received the more forceful *smashed* wording were subsequently more likely to report seeing broken glass than those given *hit* or no speed question, despite its absence from the film.

This matters because information introduced after an event can influence subsequent reports about the event itself. However, a changed answer alone does not necessarily prove that someone's internal memory was altered. Expectations about how to respond can also influence answers.

## 6. Remembering what, but not where from

Knowing a piece of information and knowing where that information came from are different cognitive achievements.

Consider three people who all believe the same statement:

> There was a blue car outside the house.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="sources" />

All three people possess the same piece of information. But their evidence is different. One saw the car, another heard about it, and a third inferred it from what usually happens.

After enough time, someone might remember the information while becoming uncertain about its original source. They may even attribute it to the wrong source. This is a **source-monitoring error**, particularly relevant when distinguishing experiences, testimony, imagination and inference. [Johnson, Hashtroudi & Lindsay (1993)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346328/)

### A tool for reasoning about origins

Separate three categories: **seen**, **inferred**, and **learned afterward**. Keep uncertainty explicit when you cannot distinguish them.

| Seen | Inferred | Learned afterward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blue bag | The bag belongs to the person. | The question mentioned an umbrella. |
| Red cup | They were in a hurry. | |
| Green book | | |

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-34" preset="source-sorting" id="memory-sources" />

If the umbrella moves from “learned afterward” to “seen,” its evidential status has changed without any new evidence about the original scene. That is the error to recognize.

Later information is not necessarily false. Its origin matters when evaluating what it establishes. Hearing someone mention an umbrella establishes that it was mentioned; it does not, by itself, establish that it was in the room.

## 7. Confidence and accuracy are different variables

A person can remember something accurately while feeling uncertain. Another person can be mistaken while feeling completely convinced.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="confidence" />

These dimensions are not necessarily unrelated. Research on eyewitness identification suggests that initial confidence can be informative under carefully controlled conditions: an initial, uncontaminated identification test, a fair lineup, no administrator influence and an immediate confidence statement. Suggestive procedures and subsequent feedback can compromise its evidential value. These findings concern particular identification procedures; they do not make every confident everyday recollection accurate. [Wixted & Wells (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100616686966)

Avoid both extremes:

| Naive position | Excessively skeptical position |
| --- | --- |
| I am certain, so it must be true. | Memory can be wrong, so no memory can be trusted. |

Memories are valuable evidence whose reliability depends partly on the circumstances under which they were formed, retained and retrieved.

**Confidence is information about your belief. It is not, by itself, a guarantee about the world.**

## 8. Investigate your own memory

Reconstruct an ordinary event from the previous day: shopping, a conversation with a friend or your journey home. Rather than trying to remember every detail, organize the information you already possess.

For each detail, ask:

1. What exactly am I claiming happened?
2. Where does this information come from?
3. How confident am I?
4. What could independently support or challenge it?

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-34" preset="worksheet" id="memory-worksheet" />

The examples are editable prompts, not claims about your day. A corroborating record must bear on the particular claim; merely listing a record does not establish that it supports the claim.

### External records are evidence, not absolute truth

Suppose a receipt shows that payment was made at 17:14. Does this establish that the person entered the supermarket at 17:14?

No. It establishes something about the time of payment, assuming the receipt is accurate. The time of arrival is a different claim.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="receipt" />

A photograph can exclude events outside its frame. A message may contain incorrect information. A timestamp can establish when one action occurred without establishing when another action occurred.

Apply the same reasoning standards to external records that you apply to memory. The objective is to compare evidence and understand what each source actually establishes.

## 9. The difference between remembering and knowing

Return one final time to the original room.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="comparison" />

The two scenes are similar enough to be recognizable, but they are not identical. The right-hand image is an illustrative reconstruction, not a measurement of your recollection.

Memory allows us to bring the past into the present. But what we bring into the present is not necessarily an exact reproduction of what happened. When something matters, we should ask not only what we remember, but how we came to remember it.

**Do you remember the event, or do you remember information about the event?**

## 10. Why this belongs in a reasoning course

The mathematical lessons primarily investigated how to move from premises to conclusions. Observation and memory also require us to investigate the reliability of the premises themselves.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-34" figure="premises" />

Even logically impeccable reasoning cannot guarantee a true conclusion if it begins with inaccurate premises. Perception, memory and the origin of our information are fundamental components of rigorous reasoning.
