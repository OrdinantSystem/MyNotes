A scientific conclusion is not an isolated statement. It is the endpoint of a chain of reasoning, measurements, comparisons and assumptions. Understanding a study means understanding that chain.

This workshop brings together tools for evaluating evidence: probability, uncertainty, measurement, experimental design, causal inference, confounding and selective reporting. We take a real scientific study and reconstruct the reasoning behind it, travelling from a popular headline to the original evidence.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="chain" />

The aim is a reusable method for reading research independently, rather than simply knowing more about this particular learning technique.

## 1. Start with the headline

The study is Jeffrey D. Karpicke and Janell R. Blunt’s 2011 paper, *Retrieval Practice Produces More Learning than Elaborative Studying with Concept Mapping*, published in *Science* 331, 772–775. The [paper](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf) and its [supplementary methods and results](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_ScienceSupportingMaterial.pdf) are available from the authors’ laboratory.

It concerns a familiar question: how should I study if I actually want to remember and understand something? Two experiments compared retrieval practice with other learning strategies on delayed assessments of science material. Multiple conditions, numerical results, subjective predictions, supplementary methods and a second experiment give us several points at which to examine the reasoning.

The paper also generated a real newspaper headline:

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="headline" />

[Pam Belluck’s news report, archived by UCLA IDEA](https://idea.gseis.ucla.edu/newsroom/education-news-roundup/archive/archive-of-education-news-roundup-for-2011/january-2011/to-really-learn-quit-studying-and-take-a-test) appeared on 20 January 2011. We will investigate how far the evidence justifies the headline, without beginning from the assumption that either the journalist or the researchers must be wrong.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="meanings" />

The headline contains concepts whose meanings are much broader than anything a single experiment could reasonably investigate. A reader could interpret it as saying that studying is ineffective, examinations are the best way to learn, or students should replace their current methods entirely. Those are different claims. They require different evidence.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="questions" />

**Before asking whether a claim is true, establish what the claim actually means.** Changing the meaning of a word can change the entire problem.

## 2. Replace the headline with a research question

Imagine two students reading the same passage about an unfamiliar scientific topic. After reading, one continues examining the material. The other closes the text and attempts to reconstruct its contents from memory.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="methods" />

**Elaborative studying** organizes and connects information while consulting the material. **Retrieval practice** attempts to recover previously studied information without consulting it. Both students are studying; they differ in what they do with the information during learning.

For the central comparison in Experiment 1, our question becomes:

> Does attempting to reconstruct a previously studied science text from memory improve performance one week later, compared with constructing a concept map while consulting that text, when learning time is matched?

This is narrower than the headline, but that narrowness makes it possible to investigate experimentally.

| Element | What was investigated? |
| --- | --- |
| Population | University undergraduate students |
| Intervention | Retrieval practice combined with reading |
| Comparison | Elaborative concept mapping with the text available |
| Outcome | Performance on a delayed assessment |
| Time | One week after learning |

As new information arrives, refine this template. A conclusion is meaningful only in relation to the population, procedure, comparison, measurement and interval that produced it.

## 3. Follow the participants

Experiment 1 involved 80 Purdue undergraduates participating for course credit, assigned to four conditions with 20 students in each. All received the same 276-word passage about sea otters. The [supplementary methods](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_ScienceSupportingMaterial.pdf) describe the participants and procedures.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="participants" />

Random assignment helps make groups comparable in expectation, reducing systematic differences in prior ability, knowledge and motivation. It does not guarantee identical groups, and it does not make these participants a representative random sample of all students. Randomizing people already inside an experiment differs from randomly selecting people from the world.

### What did the participants actually do?

The groups did not all spend the same amount of time learning.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="timing" />

The study-once group read for five minutes. The repeated-study group had four five-minute reading periods. The mapping group read for five minutes, then had 25 minutes to construct and review a concept map with the text available. The retrieval group read for five minutes, recalled for ten, reread for five, and recalled again for ten. Instructions and brief breaks are excluded from this reconstruction of active time.

**Which comparison gives the strongest evidence about the two methods, rather than simply the amount of time spent studying?**

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-48" preset="comparison" id="research48-comparison" />

Comparing study once with retrieval compares five with thirty active minutes. The more informative comparison for our question is concept mapping versus retrieval: both receive thirty minutes. Equal time still leaves differences in activity format, text access and the sequence of learning activities. This will matter when interpreting what caused the result.

## 4. What counts as learning?

The broad construct is **learning**. But learning cannot simply be placed on a scale and weighed. The researchers needed observable indicators: an operational definition.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="measurement" />

One week after learning, students answered 14 questions about information directly expressed in the text and two inference questions. The assessment offered 25 points: 21 for the direct questions and four for the inference questions. Calling the first type “verbatim” does not mean that only word-for-word memorization was assessed; both question types concerned conceptual knowledge.

The following original teaching example illustrates the difference. It does not reproduce the actual passage or experimental assessment.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="example" />

The first question asks for information that was directly presented. The second requires connecting ideas and making a new inference: poorer insulation can increase heat loss and the energy needed to maintain body temperature.

Including inference questions matters because the researchers sought to measure something beyond simple recall. But answering a small number of inference questions about a short passage is not equivalent to demonstrating comprehensive mastery of biology. The operational definition is informative without being exhaustive.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="delay" />

A strategy that produces excellent immediate performance may not produce excellent long-term retention. The timing of the measurement is part of what defines the conclusion. Here, “later” means one week, not an entire degree or a lifetime.

## 5. Read the result before interpreting it

The supplementary results combine the question types and report rounded mean performance of 45% for concept mapping and 67% for retrieval practice. The repeated-study mean was 49%; the study-once mean was 27%.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="results" />

How large is the observed difference between mapping and retrieval?

$$
67\%-45\%=22\text{ percentage points}.
$$

Relative to the concept-mapping baseline, the same result is:

$$
\frac{67-45}{45}\times100\%\approx48.9\%.
$$

This explains the approximately 50% improvement discussed in the paper. It does not mean that students gained 49 additional percentage points of the material.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-48" preset="framing" id="research48-framing" />

The underlying observations are identical. Only the reference quantity changes. Numerical framing can affect perception without changing the evidence.

### Failure to establish a difference is not equivalence

The supplementary results report that repeated study versus concept mapping was not statistically significant: $F(1,38)=0.31$. This does not establish that the methods are equally effective. A conventional difference test failing to reject its null hypothesis is not an equivalence test. A claim of practical equivalence needs a justified margin and an appropriate analysis.

### An observed effect is not an exact population effect

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="variation" />

These distributions are schematic illustrations, not reconstructed participant-level data. The group averages do not imply that every student performed better with retrieval. Individuals vary, and group means conceal that variation.

The paper reports Cohen’s $d=1.50$ for retrieval versus elaborative concept mapping in Experiment 1. The underlying idea is a difference in means relative to within-group variability:

$$
d=\frac{\bar{x}_{R}-\bar{x}_{M}}{s_{\mathrm{pooled}}}.
$$

Here $\bar{x}_{R}$ and $\bar{x}_{M}$ are the sample means and $s_{\mathrm{pooled}}$ is a pooled within-group standard deviation. The reported standardized comparison is large relative to the variability used in that calculation. We quote the reported $d$; neither the illustrative curve widths nor a raw dataset are inferred from it. The magnitude of an effect and uncertainty about its estimation are distinct questions.

### Visualizing uncertainty

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="interval" />

The observed difference is 22 percentage points. Treating it as an exact universal effect would be unjustified. Under the corresponding statistical model, the data support a positive difference while allowing uncertainty about its magnitude.

<Exercise question="Optional calculation: how was this approximate interval reconstructed?">

For a one-degree-of-freedom contrast, $t^2=F$. Using the rounded difference $\Delta=22$ and published $F(1,38)=21.63$:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\Delta)\approx\frac{22}{\sqrt{21.63}}\approx4.73.
$$

With $t_{0.975,38}\approx2.024$, the conventional interval is:

$$
22\pm2.024\cdot4.73\approx[12.4,31.6]\text{ percentage points}.
$$

This is our approximate reconstruction from rounded published summaries, not an interval reported by the authors or a full reanalysis. It assumes the same contrast and standard-error model as the published statistic. It describes sampling uncertainty under that model; it does not include every source of bias, measurement error or uncertainty about generalization. A 95% confidence procedure has 95% repeated-sampling coverage under its assumptions; it does not assign a 95% probability to this fixed population parameter after seeing the data.

</Exercise>

Statistical evidence can help distinguish an effect from random variation. It cannot independently resolve questions about measurement, experimental design or generalizability.

## 6. What caused the difference?

If researchers had merely observed students using their preferred study methods, prior knowledge or motivation could have influenced both their choices and their performance.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="causal" />

Assigning the learning conditions experimentally gives a stronger basis for causal interpretation. But distinguish two questions:

- Did the assigned learning procedure improve performance?
- Which precise psychological mechanism caused the improvement?

The experiment provides more direct evidence for the first question. Retrieval could influence memory through several processes. Equal active time does not isolate one psychological ingredient from every other procedural difference.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="mechanism" />

Establishing that an intervention works does not automatically establish exactly why it works.

## 7. Challenge an alternative explanation

Suppose retrieval prepares students especially well for short-answer tests because the learning activity resembles the final assessment. Perhaps they are better prepared for that test format without possessing more flexible knowledge. How could we investigate this possibility?

Experiment 2 involved 120 new participants. Each student used retrieval for one science text and concept mapping for another. Learning order was counterbalanced. The researchers also varied text structure and the final assessment format: 60 students completed short-answer questions; 60 constructed concept maps from memory. Each person was assessed on both studied texts.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="experiment-two" />

The learning-method comparison is within participants, while final assessment format varies between participants. Unlike Experiment 1’s thirty-minute conditions, the supplementary methods describe five minutes of reading plus twenty minutes of mapping, versus two five-minute reading periods and two seven-minute retrieval periods: 25 and 24 active minutes respectively. The main paper describes the time as exactly matched; these listed supplementary phase durations differ by one minute. We preserve that discrepancy rather than silently treating Experiment 2 as a repeat of the thirty-minute procedures.

If the original advantage were entirely explained by matching learning and testing formats, what might happen on the concept-map assessment? Make a prediction before revealing the reported results.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-48" preset="prediction" id="research48-prediction" />

The retrieval advantage persisted in both formats. The [paper](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf) reports standardized effects of $d=1.07$ on short-answer assessments and $d=1.01$ on concept-map assessments. This weakens an explanation based entirely on format matching. It does not rule out every role for task similarity, prove the two effects equal, or identify all underlying mechanisms.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="iteration" />

Good experiments help distinguish between competing explanations. They do more than collect observations that agree with an idea.

### Averages do not describe everyone

Experiment 2 supplies actual paired outcome counts in Table 1:

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="counts" />

The 101 participants with higher performance under retrieval are informative. So are the 13 with higher performance under mapping and the six ties. Neither observation invalidates the other. The paired scores do not establish that each individual would consistently benefit more from the same method in every subject and learning situation. Each person used the methods on different texts, so these are also not observed counterfactual outcomes for the identical text.

## 8. Match the claim’s scope to the evidence

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="radius" />

At the centre is the actual experiment. Broader claims require additional assumptions or evidence; the diagram’s distances are not quantitative evidence measures.

| Conclusion | Evidential status |
| --- | --- |
| The retrieval group achieved higher average scores. | Directly observed |
| The assigned retrieval procedure improved delayed performance in these experiments. | Supported causal interpretation |
| Retrieval could benefit similar students with similar material. | Generalization requiring assumptions |
| Retrieval is superior for learning mathematical proofs. | Requires domain-specific evidence |
| All students should stop using other study techniques. | Not established |

The participants came from one university. The materials were short science texts, the delay was one week, and the procedures differed in several details. The study did not investigate every possible combination of retrieval and concept mapping. Random assignment supports a comparison within the experiment; it does not erase those boundaries.

The authors explicitly recognized that a concept map could be constructed from memory, combining mapping and retrieval. The methods are not mutually exclusive.

There was a [technical comment by Mintzes and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1203698) and an [authors’ response](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_ScienceResponse.pdf) in 2011, addressing issues including training in concept mapping and meaningful learning. Read them as a methodological exchange, not as automatic evidence that the original finding should be accepted or dismissed. Blunt and Karpicke’s later [2014 study of retrieval-based concept mapping](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Blunt_Karpicke_JEDP.pdf) investigated how the techniques can be combined.

## 9. Build the evidence map

Bring the participants, conditions, timeline, assessment, results, uncertainty and limitations into one structure.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="evidence" />

The important feature is the preserved connection between the conclusion and the information supporting it. The same structure can organize another empirical study.

### Classify the inference

A statement’s evidential requirements depend on the inference it makes. A descriptive claim says what was observed. An associative claim relates variables. A causal claim attributes a difference to an intervention. An application proposes extending the finding elsewhere. These categories can overlap in ordinary language; here, select the specific inferential role foregrounded by each statement.

## 10. Write a calibrated conclusion

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-48" figure="conclusion" />

In two experiments involving university undergraduates learning short science texts, retrieval practice produced better average performance on assessments one week later than elaborative concept mapping with the text available.

Including opportunities to reconstruct knowledge from memory is a promising strategy, although its effectiveness in other contexts depends on implementation and evaluation. The revised conclusion is less dramatic and more useful: it preserves the conditions under which the evidence was obtained.

### Design the next experiment

Propose a study of whether retrieval helps students learn mathematical proofs. What population, comparison, time interval and outcome would you choose? How would you distinguish reproduction of a familiar proof from transfer of the reasoning technique?

## 11. A method for your next study

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-48" preset="checklist" id="research48-checklist" />

Checking a box records attention to a question, not proof that the evidence is sound. The checklist can guide reading in education, psychology, economics, public policy and other empirical disciplines.

A primary study investigates specific questions. A review evaluates findings across multiple investigations. The selected paper reports two experiments; [Dunlosky and colleagues’ 2013 review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) considers a broader literature on learning techniques. Recommendations should draw on accumulated evidence rather than treat a single study as definitive.

For methodological background, [OpenIntro Statistics](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) covers experimental design, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing. [Causal Inference in Statistics: A Primer](https://bayes.cs.ucla.edu/PRIMER/) develops causal effects, confounding, interventions and their assumptions.
