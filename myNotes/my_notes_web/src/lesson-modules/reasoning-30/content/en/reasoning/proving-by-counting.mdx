## 1. Something you can know without looking

Imagine that we invite thirteen strangers into a room.

You have never met any of them. You don't know their names, their ages, where they come from, or when they were born.

And yet, before any of them tells you anything about themselves, you can make a statement about these people that you know must be true.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="strangers" />

At least two of these people were born in the same month.

Not probably. Not almost certainly.

They must have been.

But how can we know something about thirteen people without examining any of them?

And perhaps more interestingly, what kind of information allows us to reach such a conclusion?

We've spent much of this course learning how to prove statements by following logical implications, examining contradictions, constructing intermediate steps and identifying properties that cannot change.

Today, we're going to explore another method of reasoning. One that looks almost suspiciously simple.

We're going to count.

But the difficult part will not be the arithmetic. It will be discovering **what we should be counting in the first place.**

## 2. Try to make the statement false

Instead of immediately explaining why the statement is true, let's try to construct a situation in which it is false.

We want thirteen people, all born in different months.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-30" preset="months" id="counting-30-months" />

We place the first person in January, the second in February, the third in March, and continue like this.

After placing twelve people, every month contains exactly one person.

So far, we've managed to avoid a shared birth month.

But there is still one person left.

Where can we put them?

We could move the person in January to February. But now February contains two people. We could rearrange everyone completely, but that doesn't create an additional month.

The problem is not that we haven't found a sufficiently clever arrangement.

The problem is that the arrangement we are looking for cannot exist.

If every month contained at most one person, then the twelve months could collectively contain at most twelve people.

But we have thirteen.

$$
 13>12
$$

And this is where something interesting happens. Notice how little we needed to know.

We didn't need to know which month was shared. We didn't need to know which two people shared it. We didn't need to assign probabilities to different birthdays.

We only needed two numbers and a restriction on how the objects could be distributed.

**We have proved existence without identifying a particular example.**

There must be a shared birth month, even though we might have absolutely no idea where it is.

## 3. Discovering the pigeonhole principle

Let's gradually remove the unnecessary details from our problem.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="pigeonhole" />

What if we had six objects and only five categories?

Exactly the same problem.

One hundred and one objects and one hundred categories?

Again, exactly the same problem.

The specific numbers don't matter. What matters is the relationship between them.

Suppose we have $n$ categories, where $n$ is a positive integer. And suppose every object belongs to exactly one of these categories.

If there are more than $n$ objects, then at least one category must contain two or more objects.

This is known as the **pigeonhole principle**.

Its name comes from imagining pigeons returning to a collection of pigeonholes. But the pigeons are irrelevant. So are the holes.

Mathematically, what we have discovered is a restriction on how objects can be assigned to categories.

$$
 \underbrace{n+1}_{\text{objects}} > \underbrace{n}_{\text{categories}}
$$

Suppose, for contradiction, that no category contains more than one object.

Then the total number of objects cannot exceed $n$.

But there are $n+1$ objects.

Therefore, at least one category must contain more than one.

Notice that this proof has the same structure as the argument we used with the thirteen people. We haven't developed a new argument for every possible number. We have identified the structure common to all of them.

And this is why choosing the categories matters so much.

Imagine that I tell you that among five integers, at least three must have the same parity.

How would you prove that?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="parity" />

If both categories contained at most two integers, we could account for at most four integers. The fifth integer must force one of the categories to contain at least three.

Once you recognize the correct categories, the argument becomes remarkably short.

Sometimes the most difficult part of a proof is not performing a logical inference.

It is discovering the representation in which that inference becomes obvious.

## 4. A different way of counting

Let's return to our room of people.

This time, we'll make the situation a little smaller. Five people are attending a meeting.

Some of them shake hands with one another. Not everyone necessarily shakes hands with everyone else, and no pair shakes hands more than once.

At the end of the meeting, we want to know how many handshakes occurred.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="network" />

Each point represents a person. Each line represents one handshake.

We could simply count the lines.

One, two, three, four, five.

There were five handshakes.

But imagine that we didn't have this complete picture. Imagine that each person only remembers how many people they shook hands with.

So we ask the five people individually.

Person A tells us they shook three hands.

Person B tells us they shook two.

Person C tells us they shook two.

Person D tells us they shook two.

And person E tells us they shook one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="degrees" />

$$
 3+2+2+2+1=10
$$

Adding these numbers together, we get ten.

But a moment ago, we counted only five handshakes.

Where did the additional five come from?

Did someone give us incorrect information? Did we count the graph incorrectly? Or are these two calculations actually answering different questions?

**Take a moment to decide what the number ten represents.**

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="totals" />

Both results are correct.

But they are counting different things.

And understanding precisely how those two things are related is going to allow us to discover something much more general.

## 5. Where does the factor of two come from?

Let's isolate just one handshake.

Person A shakes hands with person B.

When we ask A how many handshakes they participated in, this handshake contributes one to A's answer.

When we ask B, the very same handshake contributes one to B's answer.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="split" />

There was only one physical event. But that event involved two people.

And this is true for every handshake in our room.

Every handshake contributes exactly two to the sum of the individual answers. Not sometimes two. Not approximately two. Exactly two.

The reason is part of what a handshake between two people is.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-30" preset="regroup" id="counting-30-regroup" />

First representation — grouped by handshake

$$
 2+2+2+2+2=10
$$

Second representation — regrouped by person

$$
 3+2+2+2+1=10
$$

$$
 2\times5=3+2+2+2+1
$$

The same ten tokens have been reorganized. None were added or removed.

Look carefully at what we have just done.

Initially, we grouped these ten pieces of information by handshake. There were five groups, and every group contained two pieces.

Then we grouped the same information by person. Now there are five groups of different sizes.

But we haven't created anything. We haven't removed anything. We haven't changed the underlying collection.

We've simply changed the way it is organized.

So naturally, both methods must produce the same total.

This is the essential idea behind a mathematical technique called **double counting**.

We identify a collection of objects or relationships, count that collection in two different ways, and then equate the results.

Because beneath two apparently different expressions, we are counting the same thing.

## 6. From a room of people to a mathematical theorem

The interesting thing about our picture is that it no longer needs to represent people.

These points could represent cities, with lines representing roads between them. They could represent computers connected to one another, atoms connected by bonds, or people connected through mutual friendships.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="worlds" />

In mathematics, we call this structure a **graph**.

The points are called vertices, and the lines connecting them are called edges.

For now, we're considering finite, undirected graphs without self-loops or multiple edges between the same two vertices.

The number of edges connected to a vertex is called its *degree*.

So when we asked each person how many hands they had shaken, we were actually asking for the degree of the corresponding vertex.

Let's write the collection of vertices as $V$, the collection of edges as $E$, and the degree of a particular vertex $v$ as:

$$
 \deg(v)
$$

If we add together the degrees of all the vertices, we can express that sum using the following notation:

$$
 \sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)
$$

This simply means: go through every vertex in $V$, take its degree, and add those numbers together.

Now, how many times does each edge contribute to this sum?

Exactly twice, because each edge has two distinct endpoints.

Therefore:

$$
 \underbrace{\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)}_{\text{count by vertex}}=\underbrace{2|E|}_{\text{count by edge}}
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="lemma" />

**The handshaking lemma**

To make the reasoning completely precise, we can define the collection we are actually counting.

Let $I$ be the set of vertex-edge pairs in which the vertex is an endpoint of the edge.

$$
 I=\{(v,e)\in V\times E: v\text{ is an endpoint of }e\}.
$$

If we count these pairs by vertex, each vertex contributes exactly its degree:

$$
 |I|=\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v).
$$

If we count them by edge, each edge contributes exactly two pairs:

$$
 |I|=2|E|.
$$

Both expressions equal the cardinality of the same set. Consequently,

$$
 \boxed{\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=2|E|}
$$

And that is the complete proof.

We didn't verify every possible graph. We didn't require the vertices to form a particular shape or have particular degrees.

We found a relationship that must hold for every graph satisfying our assumptions.

The five people in the room were simply the environment in which we discovered it.

## 7. Using counting to prove something impossible

Now let's see what this relationship allows us to discover.

Seven people attend a meeting. At the end of the meeting, every person claims to have shaken hands with exactly three other people.

Could all seven people be telling the truth?

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="seven" />

You could try drawing the connections.

Connect the first person to three others. Then try satisfying the requirements of the remaining people.

Perhaps you'll create a situation where one person has too many connections, another too few, and you'll have to rearrange everything.

But we don't actually need to search through these possibilities anymore.

If every person participated in exactly three handshakes, then the sum of the degrees would be:

$$
 7\times3=21.
$$

However, we have just proved that this sum must equal twice the number of edges.

$$
 21=2|E|
$$

And therefore:

$$
 |E|=\frac{21}{2}=10.5.
$$

But the number of handshakes must be an integer.

You cannot have half of an edge in this graph.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="unpaired" />

21 participations cannot be divided into groups of exactly two.

Such an arrangement is impossible.

And this reveals a more general consequence of our theorem.

Since

$$
 \sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=2|E|,
$$

the sum of the degrees of any graph of this kind must be even.

Now, think about what happens when we add even and odd numbers together.

Adding an even number doesn't change parity. Adding an odd number changes it.

So if the total is even, there must have been an even number of odd contributions.

This means that every finite graph of the kind we're considering has an **even number of vertices with odd degree**.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="odd" />

$$
 \underbrace{3+1}_{\text{odd degrees}}+\underbrace{2+2+2}_{\text{even degrees}}=10
$$

Exactly two vertices have odd degree in this example.

Remember the previous episode, when we used invariants to show that certain configurations could never be reached?

Here, we have discovered another necessary restriction on what configurations are possible. This time, it comes from counting.

We began with something as ordinary as people shaking hands. And we arrived at a mathematical restriction on the structure of entire networks.

## 8. When double counting goes wrong

Double counting is a powerful technique, but there is a subtle danger.

The fact that two calculations look similar does not mean that they are counting the same collection.

Let's examine a proposed argument.

Four people participate in an activity. Every pair of people shakes hands exactly once.

Someone wants to determine how many handshakes occurred. They decide to count the handshakes involving each person, but they don't follow the same rule for everyone.

Their counting process is displayed below.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="audit" />

$$
 3+2+2+3=10
$$

Their conclusion:

$$
 \frac{10}{2}=5\text{ handshakes}
$$

They argue that every handshake involves two people. Therefore, we should divide the total by two.

The arithmetic is correct. The conclusion, however, is false.

Where exactly did the reasoning fail?

Let's reorganize their recorded handshakes, this time grouping identical handshakes together.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="multiplicity" />

We discover that some handshakes were counted twice, while others were counted only once.

The problem wasn't dividing by two.

The problem was assuming that every handshake had contributed exactly two to the total when that wasn't actually true.

The number of times an object is counted is called its **multiplicity**.

Dividing a count by two is justified when every object has been counted exactly twice. If the multiplicities are unequal, we cannot correct the entire count using that single division.

And this gives us three questions we should ask whenever we encounter a counting proof.

**Coverage**

Have we included every object that belongs to the collection?

**Duplication**

Have any objects been counted more than once?

**Multiplicity**

Does each object contribute the number of times our argument assumes?

These questions are not merely about avoiding arithmetic errors.

They are about verifying that the mathematical expressions genuinely represent the objects we claim they represent.

## 9. Can you discover a counting proof yourself?

Let's finish with one more problem.

Suppose there are $n$ people in a room, where $n$ is a positive integer.

This time, everyone shakes hands with everyone else, exactly once.

How many handshakes occur?

And rather than attempting to remember a formula, try to construct one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="complete" />

In a room of six people, each person shakes hands with five others.

Start with one person.

How many other people can they shake hands with?

There are $n$ people in total, but they cannot shake hands with themselves.

Therefore, every person shakes hands with exactly $n-1$ other people.

Since there are $n$ people, adding together their individual counts gives us:

$$
 n(n-1)
$$

But once again, every handshake has been counted twice.

So the actual number of handshakes is:

$$
 \boxed{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}
$$

$$
 \frac{6\times5}{2}=15
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="matrix" />

There is another way to see this.

If we organize all the possible handshakes into a table, the entry corresponding to A shaking hands with B is mirrored by the entry corresponding to B shaking hands with A.

They represent the same physical event.

Once we remove the diagonal, where people would shake hands with themselves, exactly half of the remaining entries represent distinct handshakes.

We have arrived at the same formula using another representation.

In combinatorics, this number is also written as:

$$
 \binom{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}.
$$

It represents the number of ways of choosing two distinct objects from a collection of $n$ objects when their order does not matter.

So although we were talking about handshakes, we have actually proved something about choosing pairs from any finite collection.

## 10. Counting is more than arithmetic

At the beginning of this episode, we discovered something about thirteen strangers without knowing anything about their individual birthdays.

Later, we discovered a restriction on entire networks without having to examine every possible network.

And finally, we obtained a formula that describes every possible number of handshakes when everyone shakes hands with everyone else exactly once.

These conclusions came from two related ideas.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-30" figure="synthesis" />

**The pigeonhole principle**

**Double counting**

The first allows us to discover what must happen because of how objects are distributed.

The second allows us to discover relationships between quantities because they describe the same underlying collection.

But behind both techniques is a deeper skill.

Whenever you encounter a mathematical problem, before asking how to calculate the answer, ask yourself what the quantities actually represent.

What are the objects? How are they organized? What relationships connect them? Could you count those relationships differently?

Sometimes, two expressions appear to have absolutely nothing in common.

Until you realize they are describing the same thing.

And at that moment, an equality is no longer something you have to memorize.

It becomes something you understand.


## Worksheet

The five questions come from the supplied worksheet. Hints other than the first, and reference answers other than the second, are added learning support.

### 1. Shared birth months

Among 25 people, what is the minimum number guaranteed to share a birth month?

<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>What would happen if every month contained at most two people?</p></details>

<Exercise>
At least three. If each of the twelve months contained at most two people, there could be at most $12\cdot2=24$ people. With 25, at least one month contains three or more. The guarantee cannot be increased to four: put three people in one month and two in each of the other eleven.
</Exercise>

### 2. Recovering the edge count

A network has vertices with degrees 2, 3, 4, 1 and 2. How many edges does it have?

<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>Count the vertex–edge incidences by vertex, then by edge.</p></details>

<Exercise>
6 edges. The degree sum is 12, and each edge contributes exactly two.

$$
|E|=\frac{2+3+4+1+2}{2}=6.
$$
</Exercise>

### 3. An odd number of odd degrees

Can a graph have exactly three vertices of odd degree?

<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>What is the parity of a sum of three odd integers and any number of even integers?</p></details>

<Exercise>
No, under the finite, undirected, simple-graph assumptions of this lesson. Three odd degrees would make the degree sum odd. The handshaking lemma makes it even. Zero, two or four odd-degree vertices are possible; the count of odd-degree vertices must be even.
</Exercise>

### 4. Everyone shakes hands

Ten people each shake hands with every other person exactly once. How many handshakes occur?

<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>Each person contributes nine participations. How many participations belong to one handshake?</p></details>

<Exercise>
Each of ten people has degree nine, giving 90 participations. Every handshake contributes exactly two, so there are $10\cdot9/2=45$ handshakes.
</Exercise>

### 5. A necessary condition is not enough

Can every vertex in a graph with six vertices have degree six?

<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>Evenness of the degree sum is necessary. How many distinct other vertices can one vertex meet?</p></details>

<Exercise>
No, for a simple graph without self-loops. Each vertex has only five other vertices available, so its degree is at most five. The proposed degree sum, $6\cdot6=36$, is even, but this necessary condition alone does not establish existence.
</Exercise>

## Extension: the generalized pigeonhole principle

If $N$ objects are distributed among $k$ categories, where $k\geq1$, then at least one category contains at least

$$
\boxed{\left\lceil\frac{N}{k}\right\rceil}
$$

objects.

Here, the ceiling notation means rounding upward to the nearest integer. We take $N$ to be a nonnegative integer and $k$ a positive integer.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-30" preset="distribution" id="counting-30-distribution" />

To understand why this holds, suppose every category contained at most

$$
\left\lceil\frac Nk\right\rceil-1
$$

objects. The total would then satisfy

$$
N\leq k\left(\left\lceil\frac Nk\right\rceil-1\right)<N,
$$

which is a contradiction. The strict inequality follows from $\lceil x\rceil-1<x$ and $k>0$, including when $N/k$ is an integer.

The principle does not say that every category will have this many objects. It guarantees that **at least one** will.
