## 1. The central idea

An instrument can be remarkably good at detecting something when it is present, yet most of its positive results can be false alarms.

This sounds contradictory. It is not.

The problem emerges when we confuse two questions that sound almost identical:

- If something is defective, how likely is the detector to flag it?
- If the detector flags something, how likely is it to be defective?

These questions concern different populations, different denominators and, consequently, different probabilities.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="direction" />

Reversing the condition changes the question. There is no general rule allowing us to reverse the probability.

## 2. The suspicious factory machine

Imagine a fictional factory that produces small mechanical components. Every component travels along a conveyor belt and passes through an automated inspection machine.

Some components contain an internal defect that cannot be seen from the outside.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="detector" />

The manufacturer tells us two things about the detector. It correctly identifies 90% of defective components, and it mistakenly flags only 10% of healthy components.

A component passes through the detector. The warning light turns on.

How likely is it that this component is actually defective?

What probability would you initially assign?

## 3. Establishing the world before introducing the detector

Imagine the factory produces exactly 1,000 components. Of these, 10 are defective and 990 are healthy.

We can temporarily give ourselves knowledge that the factory inspector does not possess: we can see which components are actually defective.

Each dot in the diagram represents one component.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="population" />

The proportion of defective components is:

$$
\frac{10}{1000}=1\%.
$$

This is our **base rate**: the proportion of the population that possesses the property we are interested in, before we receive the detector's result.

Let the event $D$ mean that a randomly selected component is defective.

$$
P(D)=0.01
$$

The complementary event, denoted $D^c$, means that the component is not defective.

$$
P(D^c)=0.99
$$

Keep the entire population in mind. Ten defective components may sound significant in isolation, but when positioned among one thousand components, their rarity becomes immediately apparent.

This initial imbalance is what will eventually produce the surprising result.

## 4. Two populations pass through the detector

Instead of applying the detector to all 1,000 components at once, separate the population into two groups.

### The defective components

There are ten defective components. The detector successfully identifies nine of them and misses one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="sensitivity" />

Write $+$ for the event that the detector flags a component. This is called the detector's sensitivity, or true-positive rate.

$$
P(+\mid D)=90\%.
$$

The vertical bar means *given that*.

Read the expression from right to left in terms of the reasoning procedure: we first restrict our attention to defective components, and then ask how many receive a positive detector result.

### The healthy components

Now consider the 990 healthy components.

The machine mistakenly flags 10% of them.

$$
990\cdot0.10=99
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="false-positives" />

This is the false-positive rate.

$$
P(+\mid D^c)=10\%.
$$

Notice what has happened. The detector makes proportionally fewer mistakes than correct detections, but it has far more opportunities to make mistakes among the healthy components.

A small fraction of a large group can be much larger than a large fraction of a small group.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="counts" />

## 5. Changing the reference group

Until now, we have organized the components according to their actual condition: defective or healthy. But the factory inspector does not know their actual condition. The inspector only knows whether the detector flagged them.

We must therefore reorganize exactly the same population according to a different property.

No components are added, removed or modified. Only their grouping changes.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-37" preset="regroup" id="detector-regroup" />

We are left with 108 flagged components.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="flagged" />

Now we can finally answer the question from the beginning.

If a component is flagged, what is the probability that it is actually defective?

$$
\boxed{P(D\mid +)=\frac{9}{108}\approx8.3\%}
$$

Despite the detector correctly identifying 90% of defective components, approximately 92% of its positive results are false alarms in this particular factory population.

There is no contradiction.

The two percentages describe different reference groups.

## 6. Conditional probability as a restriction of the world

When we learn that an event has occurred, we restrict our attention to the outcomes compatible with that information.

$$
P(D\mid +)=\frac{P(D\cap +)}{P(+)}
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="restriction" />

The intersection $D\cap+$ represents all components that are simultaneously defective and flagged.

The denominator represents all components that are flagged, regardless of their actual condition. Conditional probability requires this conditioning event to have positive probability: $P(+)>0$.

This is the same intersection we considered when calculating the detector's sensitivity. However, the denominator has changed.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="denominators" />

The numerator has remained nine throughout the regrouping.

It is the denominator that changes everything.

This connects with the earlier lesson on conditional statements. Just as $A\implies B$ does not generally establish $B\implies A$, knowing $P(B\mid A)$ does not automatically establish $P(A\mid B)$.

The logical and probabilistic situations are different, but both require us to pay attention to the direction of a relationship.

## 7. Discovering Bayes' theorem rather than memorizing it

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="tree" />

There are only two possible ways for a component to receive a positive result. It can be defective and correctly flagged, or healthy and incorrectly flagged.

The probability of the first path is:

$$
P(D\cap+)=P(D)P(+\mid D).
$$

The probability of the second path is:

$$
P(D^c\cap+)=P(D^c)P(+\mid D^c).
$$

Because these two events are mutually exclusive and together include every positive result, their probabilities can be added.

$$
P(+)=P(D)P(+\mid D)+P(D^c)P(+\mid D^c).
$$

Substitute these expressions into the definition of conditional probability.

### Bayes' theorem

$$
\displaystyle P(D\mid+)=\frac{P(+\mid D)P(D)}{P(+)}
$$

Expanding the denominator into both possible paths

$$
\displaystyle P(D\mid+)=\frac{P(+\mid D)P(D)}{P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid D^c)P(D^c)}
$$

The factory example becomes:

$$
P(D\mid+)= \frac{0.9\cdot0.01} {0.9\cdot0.01+0.1\cdot0.99}.
$$

$$
=\frac{0.009}{0.009+0.099} =\frac{9}{108}.
$$

$$
\boxed{P(D\mid+)\approx0.0833}
$$

The formula is not a new fact disconnected from everything we have done before. It is simply a compact mathematical description of the process that we have already seen.

We separated the population, followed two paths, reunited the flagged components, and calculated their relative proportions.

## 8. What happens when we change the world, but not the detector?

Now return to the factory. Imagine that a manufacturing problem causes defective components to become more common.

Nothing about the detector has changed. Its sensitivity remains 90%, and its false-positive rate remains 10%.

Only the composition of the population changes.

Before changing the base rate, predict whether the probability that a flagged component is defective will increase, decrease or remain constant.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-37" preset="base-rate" id="detector-base-rate" />

The results are quite remarkable.

| Base rate of defects | Correct detections | False alarms | Probability of defect if flagged |
| -------------------- | ------------------ | ------------ | -------------------------------- |
| 1%                   | 9                  | 99           | 8.3%                             |
| 5%                   | 45                 | 95           | 32.1%                            |
| 10%                  | 90                 | 90           | 50%                              |
| 20%                  | 180                | 80           | 69.2%                            |
| 50%                  | 450                | 50           | 90%                              |

The very same detector can produce mostly false alarms in one factory and mostly correct positive results in another.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="curve" />

This reveals a deeper insight: the meaning of evidence depends not only on the mechanism that produced it, but also on the world in which that mechanism operates.

A detector result cannot always be interpreted independently of its context.

## 9. Doubtful evidence is not worthless evidence

If only 8.3% of flagged components are defective, does that mean the detector is useless?

Not necessarily.

Before using the detector, the probability that a randomly selected component was defective was only 1%.

After receiving a positive result, that probability increased to approximately 8.3%.

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="update" />

The result provides genuine information. A flagged component is substantially more suspicious than a randomly selected component.

But becoming more likely is not the same thing as becoming likely.

This distinction is particularly valuable for the broader course. Evidence can rationally increase our confidence in a conclusion without establishing that conclusion as true or even more probable than its alternative.

The detector may therefore be useful as an initial screening mechanism, followed by a more precise inspection procedure.

Whether that is worthwhile depends on the costs of inspection, missed defects and false alarms.

## 10. Reconstructing the reasoning

Consider a different fictional factory.

This factory produces 2,000 components. Of these, 5% are defective. Its detector identifies 80% of defective components and mistakenly flags 5% of healthy ones.

Calculate the probability that a flagged component is actually defective.

<Interactive kind="module" module="reasoning-37" preset="workshop" id="detector-workshop" />

The purpose of this exercise is not to test whether you remember Bayes' theorem. It is to determine whether you can reconstruct the necessary reference group.

Someone who solves this problem by drawing circles and counting them has understood the essential concept just as well as someone who uses the formula.

## 11. Every probability has a reference group

<ReasoningFigure kind="module" module="reasoning-37" figure="summary" />

All three statements are true simultaneously. None contradicts the others.

Whenever someone presents a probability, an accuracy percentage or a success rate, one of the most important questions we can ask is deceptively simple:

**A percentage of what?**

Look once more at the denominator of the final fraction.

$$
\frac{9}{\boxed{108}}
$$

## Model assumptions and further reading

The rates and counts here are exact assumptions of fictional factory models. In observed populations, frequencies can fluctuate, and detector performance need not stay constant across settings. The base-rate comparison deliberately holds both detector rates fixed. None of these examples supplies performance data for a real detector.

[OpenIntro Statistics](https://www.openintro.org/book/os/) provides further reading on conditional probability and probability trees.

[Matthew Knachel — Fundamental Methods of Logic](https://open.umn.edu/opentextbooks/textbooks/fundamental-methods-of-logic), Chapter 6, connects probability with reasoning.

[Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage (1995), How to Improve Bayesian Reasoning Without Instruction: Frequency Formats](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684) is the supplied companion reading on natural-frequency representations. The counts-first approach here does not assert a measured learning outcome for this lesson.
