## 1. The number that cannot exist

Imagine that someone tells you they have discovered the largest integer.

Not the largest number that a computer can store. Not the largest number that anyone has written down. The largest integer that can possibly exist.

Let's call this mysterious number $N$.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-largest" />

If this person is correct, there cannot be an integer larger than $N$. We have reached the end.

But there is something strange about this claim. We already know how addition works. Nothing prevents us from taking this number and adding one to it.

$$
N+1
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-successor" />

And now we have an integer larger than the largest integer.

Something has gone wrong.

But notice where the problem appeared. We did not make a mistake when adding one. We did not change the rules of mathematics. We simply followed the consequences of accepting the original claim.

The problem was the claim itself.

And this gives us a surprisingly powerful way of reasoning.

Instead of trying to prove something directly, we can temporarily accept its opposite and investigate the mathematical world that follows from it.

If that world cannot possibly exist, neither can the assumption that produced it.

## 2. Building a world that cannot exist

Let's examine what actually happened in our argument.

When we assumed that $N$ was the largest integer, we accepted something very specific. Every integer must be smaller than or equal to $N$.

Mathematically, we can express this as:

$$
\forall m\in\mathbb Z,\quad m\leq N.
$$

This is not an additional assumption. It is what being the largest integer means.

But the integers are closed under addition. Since both $N$ and $1$ are integers, their sum must also be an integer.

$$
N\in\mathbb Z \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N+1\in\mathbb Z.
$$

And because $N$ was supposedly larger than or equal to every integer, this must also apply to $N+1$.

Therefore:

$$
N+1\leq N.
$$

At the same time, from the ordinary properties of numbers, we know that:

$$
N+1>N.
$$

Now place these two conclusions beside each other.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-integer-conflict" />

These statements cannot simultaneously be true. One says that $N+1$ is greater than $N$. The other says that it is not.

This is a genuine logical incompatibility.

And because our reasoning was valid, and the other mathematical properties we used were already accepted, we must reject the temporary assumption.

$$
\boxed{\text{There is no largest integer.}}
$$

The contradiction is not the result we wanted to prove. It is the evidence that the opposite of our desired result cannot hold.

This method is called **proof by contradiction**, sometimes referred to by its Latin name, *reductio ad absurdum*.

## 3. Why does this method work?

Imagine that we have a mathematical proposition $P$ that we want to prove.

In a direct proof, we begin with what we already know and try to construct a path toward $P$.

But in a proof by contradiction, we do something different. We temporarily introduce a new assumption:

$$
\neg P.
$$

We are not claiming that this assumption is true. We are investigating what would follow if it were true.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-eliminate" />

Suppose that, after a sequence of valid deductions, we arrive at two incompatible statements:

$$
Q\qquad\text{and}\qquad\neg Q.
$$

It does not particularly matter what $Q$ is. It could be a statement about numbers, sets, functions, geometry, or something else entirely.

What matters is that the two statements cannot both be true.

We represent this logical impossibility with the symbol:

$$
\bot
$$

This symbol is sometimes called *bottom*, and in this context it denotes falsity or contradiction.

The entire structure of our reasoning can therefore be represented as:

$$
\neg P\Longrightarrow\bot.
$$

If the negation of $P$ necessarily leads to a contradiction, then the negation of $P$ cannot be true, given our accepted premises.

Consequently, in classical logic:

$$
\neg\neg P\Longrightarrow P.
$$

And we have established the proposition we originally wanted to prove.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-architecture" />

There is an important detail here.

A contradiction does not automatically tell us which of several assumptions is false. It tells us that they cannot all be true together.

In a mathematical proof, we isolate the assumption under investigation by keeping the other premises fixed and ensuring that every deduction is valid.

That is what allows us to identify precisely what must be rejected.

## 4. Strange does not mean impossible

Before we use this technique to prove something more interesting, we need to understand what a contradiction actually is.

Because human beings have a tendency to confuse things that seem impossible with things that merely seem strange.

Imagine someone presents the following statement:

*It is possible for two positive integers to have a product that is equal to their sum.*

At first, this might appear unreasonable.

Multiplication usually produces larger numbers than addition does when we work with sufficiently large positive integers.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-operations" />

But what happens if we actually investigate the possibility?

Suppose that the two numbers are both equal to two.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-equal-operations" />

The result may be unexpected, but there is nothing logically impossible about it.

Now compare these three situations.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-comparison" />

Only the third situation establishes a logical impossibility.

This distinction becomes particularly important when we apply reasoning outside mathematics.

The fact that an idea leads to a conclusion we dislike, or one that challenges our expectations, is not sufficient reason to reject it.

A proof by contradiction requires more than discomfort. It requires incompatibility.

And now we are going to use this distinction to investigate a question that mathematicians have been studying for thousands of years.

## 5. Could we collect every prime number?

Prime numbers are among the most fundamental objects in mathematics.

A prime number is a positive integer greater than one whose only positive divisors are one and itself.

The number two is prime. Three is prime. Four is not, because we can divide it by two. Five is prime.

As we continue exploring the positive integers, we encounter more and more of them.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-prime-grid" />

But something interesting happens. Prime numbers do not appear at regular intervals.

Sometimes they are very close to each other. Sometimes we have to travel considerably further to find another one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-prime-gaps" />

This gives rise to an interesting question.

What if, somewhere unimaginably far along the number line, we encounter the last prime number?

What if after that point, every integer can be decomposed into smaller factors?

We could use a computer to search for more primes. We could discover thousands, millions, or billions of them.

But no matter how far we search, we would still have the same problem.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-search-limit" />

Discovering another prime tells us that our previous list was incomplete. But it does not prove that every possible finite list will be incomplete.

We need something more powerful than searching.

We need a mathematical argument.

So instead of trying to find infinitely many prime numbers, we are going to temporarily assume that there are only finitely many.

We are going to construct a world in which every prime number has already been discovered.

And then we will try to escape that world using its own mathematical rules.

## 6. Constructing the escape

To understand how we might escape, let's first work with a small collection of prime numbers:

$$
2,\quad3,\quad5.
$$

We want to construct a number that cannot be divided evenly by any of these three primes.

We could start guessing. But instead, let's construct such a number deliberately.

First, multiply our three primes together.

$$
M=2\times3\times5=30.
$$

The number thirty has a particularly useful property. It is divisible by every prime in our collection.

We can visualize this by arranging thirty objects into groups.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-groups" />

Now we perform the simplest possible transformation.

We add one.

$$
M+1=31.
$$

What happens to each of our arrangements?

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-plus-one" />

In every case, we are left with exactly one additional object.

Adding one changes the number from $M$ to $M+1$: the original product was divisible by all three primes; the new number is divisible by none of them.

$$
\begin{aligned} 31&=2(15)+1,\\ 31&=3(10)+1,\\ 31&=5(6)+1. \end{aligned}
$$

Using the divisibility notation:

$$
2\nmid31,\qquad 3\nmid31,\qquad 5\nmid31.
$$

The symbol $\nmid$ simply means *does not divide*.

But notice something even more interesting. Thirty-one is itself a prime number.

We started with a collection of three primes and constructed a number outside that collection.

Could this be a general method for discovering new primes?

<Interactive kind="contradiction" preset="prime-builder" id="contradiction-prime-builder" />

The experiment reveals a pattern that appears to hold regardless of how many primes we include.

But it also reveals something that we need to investigate carefully.

## 7. The beautiful mistake

At this point, we might be tempted to make the following argument.

Whenever we multiply a collection of prime numbers and add one, we obtain a new prime number.

If that were true, we could generate prime numbers indefinitely.

And we have several examples that seem to confirm our idea.

| Collection     | Product + 1 | Result |
| -------------- | ----------- | ------ |
| 2              | 3           | Prime  |
| 2, 3           | 7           | Prime  |
| 2, 3, 5        | 31          | Prime  |
| 2, 3, 5, 7     | 211         | Prime  |
| 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 | 2311        | Prime  |

Five successful experiments.

But let's include another prime.

$$
2\times3\times5\times7\times11\times13+1
$$

$$
=30031.
$$

This number looks promising. It is not divisible by two, three, five, seven, eleven, or thirteen.

So is it prime?

**Before reading on:** does excluding the six listed divisors establish that $30031$ is prime?

$$
\boxed{30031=59\times509}
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-factorization" />

Our conjecture was wrong.

But our original observation remains completely valid.

The number we constructed is not divisible by any prime in our collection.

We never established that it was divisible by no other prime numbers.

Those are two very different statements.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-established" />

And here is where our apparent failure becomes something much more interesting.

We do not actually need the constructed number to be prime.

We only need it to have a prime divisor that was not in our original collection.

And every integer greater than one has at least one prime divisor.

If the integer is itself prime, it is its own prime divisor. Otherwise, we can find smaller factors greater than one and continue factoring until we reach a prime.

A strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers cannot continue indefinitely, so this factorization reaches a prime divisor.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-prime-divisor" />

Either way, we obtain exactly what we need.

We now have all the ingredients necessary to prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

## 8. Euclid's argument — proving the infinitude of primes

We are now going to leave behind our particular examples.

Instead of working with two, three, five, or any particular collection, we are going to work with an arbitrary finite collection of primes.

Our desired result is:

$$
P:\text{ There are infinitely many primes.}
$$

We begin by assuming its negation.

$$
\neg P:\text{ There are only finitely many primes.}
$$

If there are only finitely many primes, we can theoretically place all of them into one complete list.

$$
p_1,p_2,p_3,\ldots,p_n.
$$

We do not need to know how many primes there are. We do not need to know how large they are.

We simply assume that this list contains every prime number that exists.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-all-primes" />

Now repeat the operation we discovered earlier.

Multiply every prime in the list together:

$$
M=p_1p_2p_3\cdots p_n.
$$

And construct the number:

$$
N=M+1.
$$

Or equivalently:

$$
\boxed{N=p_1p_2\cdots p_n+1.}
$$

Every prime in our list divides $M$.

For any prime $p_i$ in the collection, we can write:

$$
M=p_i k_i
$$

for some integer $k_i$.

Consequently,

$$
N=p_i k_i+1.
$$

The remainder when dividing $N$ by $p_i$ is one.

Therefore:

$$
p_i\nmid N.
$$

And because nothing in our reasoning depends on which prime we selected, this is true for every prime in our list.

$$
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}, \quad p_i\nmid N.
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-none-divide" />

But we also know something else.

The number $N$ is greater than one. Therefore, it must have at least one prime divisor.

Let's call that prime divisor $q$.

$$
q\mid N.
$$

We know that $q$ is prime.

And because none of the primes in our collection divides $N$, we know that $q$ cannot be any of them.

$$
q\neq p_1,\qquad q\neq p_2,\qquad \ldots,\qquad q\neq p_n.
$$

And now something impossible has happened.

We have discovered a prime number that does not belong to the collection of all prime numbers.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-outside" />

Let's isolate the exact contradiction.

If our original assumption were true, every prime number would belong to the list.

Since $q$ is prime:

$$
q\in\{p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\}.
$$

But our construction proves that:

$$
q\notin\{p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\}.
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-prime-conflict" />

The two statements cannot simultaneously be true.

Therefore, our original assumption must be false.

$$
\boxed{\text{There are infinitely many prime numbers.}}
$$

What makes this proof so remarkable is that we did not need to discover infinitely many primes.

We did not even need to know a formula that generates prime numbers.

We proved that every proposed finite collection of all primes necessarily fails to contain them all.

No matter how large the collection becomes, it can never be complete.

<Interactive kind="contradiction" preset="euclid" id="contradiction-euclid" />

### The finished mathematical proof

*The previous investigation can now be compressed into a rigorous written argument. This is the transition from discovering a proof to presenting one, as established in earlier lessons.*

**Theorem.** There are infinitely many prime numbers.

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes. Let

$$
p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n
$$

be a list containing all prime numbers.

Define

$$
N=p_1p_2\cdots p_n+1.
$$

For every $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$,

$$
N\equiv1\pmod{p_i}.
$$

Hence,

$$
p_i\nmid N.
$$

Since $N>1$, there exists a prime $q$ such that

$$
q\mid N.
$$

Therefore, $q\neq p_i$ for every $i$. In particular,

$$
q\notin\{p_1,\ldots,p_n\}.
$$

But $q$ is prime, contradicting the assumption that the list contains all prime numbers.

Thus there are infinitely many primes. $\square$

The underlying argument appears in Euclid's Elements, Book IX, Proposition 20. Euclid's original presentation constructs a prime outside an assigned finite collection; the version above uses a modern proof-by-contradiction formulation.

## 9. Three different ways of reaching the truth

We have now encountered three important approaches to proving mathematical statements.

A direct proof begins with the available assumptions and constructs a path toward the conclusion.

A proof by contraposition reverses and negates the two components of an implication.

And a proof by contradiction temporarily accepts the negation of the desired result and investigates its consequences.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-methods" />

These are not three different kinds of mathematical truth. They are three different routes for establishing truth.

In the case of a conditional statement,

$$
A\Longrightarrow B,
$$

proof by contradiction begins by assuming that $A$ is true and $B$ is false, because this is precisely the situation in which the original implication would fail.

$$
\neg(A\Rightarrow B) \equiv A\land\neg B.
$$

Sometimes these proof methods overlap. A contrapositive proof can often be adapted into a proof by contradiction.

The important skill is learning which route gives you the clearest mathematical argument.

## 10. Your turn — find the impossible world

Let's finish by applying the method ourselves.

Consider the following proposition:

**There is no smallest positive real number.**

At first, this might sound strange. Positive numbers can become incredibly small.

But could there be one that is smaller than all the others?

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-smallest" />

Assume that $r$ is the smallest positive real number.

What mathematical operation could we perform to construct another positive real number smaller than $r$?

<Interactive kind="contradiction" preset="smallest" id="contradiction-smallest" />

Since $r>0$, we have:

$$
0<\frac r2<r.
$$

But if $r$ is the smallest positive real number, every positive real number must be greater than or equal to $r$.

Therefore,

$$
\frac r2\geq r.
$$

Once again, we arrive at two incompatible statements.

$$
\frac r2<r \qquad\land\qquad \frac r2\geq r.
$$

Consequently, our temporary assumption is false.

$$
\boxed{\text{There is no smallest positive real number.}}
$$

Notice the similarity between this proof and the argument with which we began.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-extremes" />

Different mathematical objects. Different operations. The same underlying architecture of reasoning.

## 11. What impossibility can teach us

At the beginning of this episode, we imagined a world containing a largest integer.

Then we imagined a world containing only finitely many prime numbers.

And finally, a world containing a smallest positive real number.

In each case, we temporarily accepted the possibility. We followed its consequences. And eventually, we discovered that the world we had imagined could not consistently exist.

<ReasoningFigure kind="contradiction-synthesis" />

But perhaps the most important lesson is not about integers or prime numbers.

It is about the relationship between an idea and its consequences.

An idea might appear convincing when we first encounter it. But accepting an idea means accepting everything that logically follows from it.

And sometimes, it is only by exploring those consequences that we can understand whether the idea was possible in the first place.

**Sometimes, to understand why something must be true, we need to understand why it cannot be false.**

## Additional exercises

### 1. The largest real number

Prove that there is no largest real number.

<details><summary>Show hint</summary>Assume a largest real number $R$ exists. What happens to $R+1$?</details>

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** Suppose $R$ is the largest real number. Closure under addition gives $R+1\in\mathbb R$, so maximality implies $R+1\leq R$. But $1>0$ implies $R+1>R$, a contradiction. Thus no largest real number exists.
</Exercise>

### 2. Repair the argument

Someone claims that $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13+1$ is prime because none of the six primes divides it. Identify the error.

<details><summary>Show hint</summary>Have all possible prime divisors been tested?</details>

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** The test excludes only the six listed primes. In fact, $30031=59\cdot509$, where both factors are prime. The valid conclusion is that no listed prime divides the constructed number; its prime divisors need not be listed.
</Exercise>

### 3. A conditional statement

Prove that if $a,b\in\mathbb Z$ and $ab$ is odd, then both $a$ and $b$ are odd.

<details><summary>Show hint</summary>Keep the odd-product assumption and negate “both are odd” using De Morgan's law.</details>

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** Suppose $ab$ is odd and at least one factor is even. If $a=2k$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$, then $ab=2(kb)$ is even because $kb\in\mathbb Z$. If $b=2k$, then $ab=2(ak)$ is even because $ak\in\mathbb Z$. These cases cover the negated conclusion, including when both factors are even. The product would be both odd and even, a contradiction. Therefore both factors are odd.
</Exercise>

### 4. Between two numbers

Prove that there cannot be two real numbers $a<b$ with no real number strictly between them.

<details><summary>Show hint</summary>Consider the midpoint and compare its distance from each endpoint.</details>

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** Suppose $a,b\in\mathbb R$, $a<b$, and no real number lies strictly between them. Set $c=(a+b)/2$. Then $c\in\mathbb R$ and $c-a=(b-a)/2>0$, while $b-c=(b-a)/2>0$. Hence $a<c<b$, contradicting the assumption. Thus every pair of distinct real endpoints has a real number strictly between them.
</Exercise>

### 5. Arbitrarily large primes

Prove that for every positive integer $n$, there is a prime greater than $n$.

<details><summary>Show hint</summary>Construct $n!+1$. A prime divisor of it cannot be at most $n$.</details>

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** Let $n\in\mathbb Z$ with $n\geq1$. Suppose no prime exceeds $n$. Since $n!+1>1$, it has a prime divisor $q$. Our assumption gives $2\leq q\leq n$, so $q$ is a factor of $n!$. Thus $q$ divides both $n!+1$ and $n!$, hence also their difference $1$, which is impossible for a prime. Therefore a prime greater than $n$ exists. If $n=1$, the assumed bound already contradicts $q\geq2$; equivalently, the prime $2$ supplies the required example.
</Exercise>
