## 1. A surprisingly difficult question

Imagine you're at a gathering with a group of people. You don't necessarily know everyone there, but there's one thing you can say with confidence:

**Every person knows at least one person.**

Now consider a second statement:

**There is someone whom every person knows.**

At first, these might sound like two ways of expressing roughly the same thing. After all, if everybody knows somebody, doesn't that mean there must be someone everybody knows?

Not necessarily.

In fact, those two sentences describe very different situations. And understanding precisely *why* they're different will reveal something fundamental about mathematical reasoning: the order in which we make choices can completely change the meaning of a statement.

Let's investigate with just three people.

## 2. A tiny world of three people

Imagine three people: Alice, Ben, and Clara.

Alice knows Ben. Ben knows Clara. And Clara knows Alice.

That's all the acquaintance relationships we'll assume for now.

<Interactive kind="dependence" preset="network" id="acquaintance-network" />

Each arrow points from the person who knows someone to the person they know. So an arrow from Alice to Ben means Alice knows Ben.

Notice that these arrows have a direction. Alice knowing Ben doesn't automatically mean Ben knows Alice. We are describing the relationships shown in this little world, rather than assuming that acquaintance must always be mutual.

There's one more convention worth making explicit: we won't assume someone knows themselves unless the diagram has a loop pointing back to that same person. Our model allows such loops, but the first network has none.

Now consider the original question: does everyone here know someone?

Well, Alice knows Ben. Ben knows Clara. And Clara knows Alice.

So yes. Every person has at least one outgoing arrow.

But there's something interesting about how we've established that. We didn't find one person whom everybody knows. Instead, we found **a different person for each person we examined**.

That difference is the whole mystery.

## 3. Translating the network into mathematics

Mathematics gives us a language for expressing relationships like these precisely.

Let's introduce the expression

$$
 K(x,y)
$$

and read it as:

"Person $x$ knows person $y$."

Here, $K$ stands for our acquaintance relationship. The letters $x$ and $y$ are variables representing people.

For example,

$$
 K(A,B)
$$

means Alice knows Ben, which is true in our network.

But

$$
 K(B,A)
$$

means Ben knows Alice. According to the arrows we've drawn, that's false.

This is called a **predicate**, or more precisely, a two-place predicate. Once we choose values for its two variables, it expresses a proposition that can be true or false.

We also need to decide which people our variables can represent. That collection is called our *domain*.

For now, the domain is simply

$$
 U=\{A,B,C\}.
$$

So when we say "everyone," we mean everyone in this three-person group. And when we say "someone," we mean at least one person from this same group.

Now we're ready for two new symbols.

<ReasoningFigure kind="quantifier-symbols" />

The first symbol, $\forall$, is called the *universal quantifier*. It means "for every" or "for all."

The second symbol, $\exists$, is called the *existential quantifier*. It means "there exists" or "for at least one."

These symbols are called quantifiers because they tell us how extensively a condition must hold across our domain.

With this notation, the statement "Everyone knows someone" becomes

$$
 \boxed{\forall x\;\exists y\;K(x,y)}
$$

Read it carefully, from left to right.

For every person $x$, there exists a person $y$, such that $x$ knows $y$.

What matters is that the first quantifier tells us to consider every possible $x$. Once we've selected a particular $x$, the second quantifier allows us to find some $y$ that works for that choice.

Let's test it against our original network.

## 4. Everyone knows someone

We can think of the universal quantifier as giving us three cases to check: Alice, Ben, and Clara.

The existential quantifier then asks whether we can find at least one suitable acquaintance in each case.

| Person $x$ | Choice of $y$ | $K(x,y)$ |
| -------------- | ----------------- | ------------ |
| Alice          | Ben               |  True   |
| Ben            | Clara             |  True   |
| Clara          | Alice             |  True   |

Every row passes. That means

$$
 \forall x\;\exists y\;K(x,y)
$$

is true in our network.

But look at the second column. Our choice of $y$ changes depending on who $x$ is.

For Alice, the chosen acquaintance is Ben. For Ben, it's Clara. And for Clara, it's Alice.

There is nothing wrong with that. The statement never required us to use the same $y$ every time.

In fact, we can represent the choices with a little rule:

$$
y(x)=\begin{cases}B&\text{if }x=A,\\C&\text{if }x=B,\\A&\text{if }x=C.\end{cases}
$$

Here, $y(x)$ simply records a witness we chose for each person $x$. We're not claiming that everyone has only one acquaintance. We just need one that works for each person.

This gives us a powerful way to understand our first statement:

**For every input $x$, we can find a suitable $y$.**

<ReasoningFigure kind="dependent-choices" />

Now, let's change the question.

Instead of asking whether everyone can find somebody they know, suppose we ask:

**Can we find one person whom everybody knows?**

That sounds similar, but notice what happens to the mathematics.

## 5. Someone whom everyone knows

The new statement is

$$
 \boxed{\exists y\;\forall x\;K(x,y)}
$$

We've changed only the order of the two quantifiers.

Now we begin by choosing a single person $y$. After making that choice, we must verify that every person $x$ knows that same $y$.

We are no longer allowed to change our chosen $y$ from one person to the next.

Let's try.

Suppose we choose Alice as our candidate. Clara knows Alice, but Ben doesn't. So Alice fails.

What about Ben? Alice knows Ben, but Clara doesn't. So Ben fails too.

And Clara? Ben knows Clara, but Alice doesn't. Another failure.

| Candidate known by everyone | A person who does not know them |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Alice                       | Ben                             |
| Ben                         | Clara                           |
| Clara                       | Alice                           |

We've checked every possible candidate, and each one fails.

Therefore,

$$
 \exists y\;\forall x\;K(x,y)
$$

is false in our original network.

This is our first major discovery.

<ReasoningFigure kind="quantifier-orders" />

The first statement is true, and the second is false, even though they use exactly the same predicate, variables, and quantifiers.

Only the order changed.

And because we have found a situation in which they have different truth values, we have demonstrated that the two statements are **not logically equivalent**.

## 6. What happens if everyone knows the same person?

Now return to our interactive network and switch from "Different contacts" to "One shared contact."

In that version, Alice knows Ben, Clara knows Ben, and Ben has a loop indicating that he knows himself.

We can select Ben first, then check each person in turn.

For Alice, $K(A,B)$ is true.

For Ben, $K(B,B)$ is true.

For Clara, $K(C,B)$ is true.

This time the very same person works for every case. The stronger statement succeeds:

$$
 \exists y\;\forall x\;K(x,y).
$$

And something else follows immediately. If everybody knows Ben, then surely everybody knows *somebody*.

That means the stronger statement guarantees the weaker one.

$$
 \boxed{ (\exists y\;\forall x\;K(x,y)) \;\Longrightarrow\; (\forall x\;\exists y\;K(x,y)) }
$$

The implication works in this direction, but not backward, as our original network already demonstrated.

This is an important form of reasoning in mathematics. We haven't just compared two sentences. We've discovered a relationship between them.

One statement is strong enough to guarantee the other, but the other doesn't contain enough information to guarantee it back.

There's a subtle point about our model worth noticing, too.

If we had defined knowing someone as a relationship that *strictly excludes knowing oneself*, then the literal sentence $\exists y\forall x K(x,y)$ would actually be impossible for a group where $x$ and $y$ range over the same people. Whoever we choose as $y$ would have to know themselves, because they're also one of the possible values of $x$.

In ordinary conversation, saying that someone is "known by everyone" often means everyone *else*. We could formalize that more precisely by writing

$$
 \exists y\;\forall x\;(x\neq y\to K(x,y)).
$$

In words: there exists a person $y$ such that every person other than $y$ knows $y$.

So mathematical precision isn't only about choosing the right symbols. It's also about defining the domain and relationship we're discussing.

## 7. The deeper idea: dependence

We've been talking about people, but the underlying idea is far more general.

Consider these two expressions again:

$$
 \forall x\;\exists y\;P(x,y)
$$

$$
 \exists y\;\forall x\;P(x,y).
$$

Here, $P(x,y)$ could describe almost any relationship: a student solving a problem, a customer receiving a service, a number being larger than another number, or a book citing a source.

The first expression allows our chosen $y$ to **depend on $x$**.

The second demands that we choose $y$ independently of $x$, and that one choice must work everywhere.

Let's see how powerful that distinction becomes when the people are replaced with numbers.

Consider:

"For every real number $x$, there is a real number $y$ greater than $x$."

Symbolically,

$$
 \forall x\in\mathbb R\;\exists y\in\mathbb R: y>x.
$$

Is that true?

Of course. Whatever real number you give me, I can choose a larger one. If you give me 5, I can choose 6. If you give me 100, I can choose 101.

In fact, I can use the general rule $y=x+1$.

But what if we reverse the quantifiers?

$$
 \exists y\in\mathbb R\;\forall x\in\mathbb R: y>x.
$$

Now we're claiming that there exists one real number larger than every real number.

That would have to be the greatest real number.

And no such number exists. Whatever candidate $y$ you choose, I can find a larger number $x=y+1$, which makes the inequality $y>x$ false.

<ReasoningFigure kind="real-choices" />

Notice how we have transferred the same logical insight from a social network to the infinite set of real numbers.

The objects changed. The relationship changed. But the structure of the reasoning stayed exactly the same.

That's one reason mathematical logic is so useful: it allows us to recognize the same pattern underneath completely different problems.

## 8. What does it mean for a quantified statement to be false?

Let's take this one step further.

So far, we've mostly been testing whether quantified statements are true. But mathematical proofs often require us to determine what it would mean for one of these statements to be false.

And this is where the quantifiers become especially important.

Suppose a teacher makes the following claim:

"Every student solved at least one problem."

Let's introduce the predicate $S(s,p)$, meaning student $s$ solved problem $p$.

Our claim becomes

$$
 \boxed{\forall s\;\exists p\;S(s,p)}
$$

For every student $s$, there exists at least one problem $p$ that student solved.

Now imagine we want to challenge this claim.

What evidence would we need?

Would it be enough to find a student who failed to solve one of the problems?

No. The teacher only claimed that each student solved *at least one*. A student could fail nine problems and still satisfy the claim by solving the tenth.

Would it be enough to find a problem that nobody solved?

Again, not necessarily. Every student might have solved a different problem.

To show that the teacher's statement is false, we need something more specific.

We need to find **one student who didn't solve any problem at all**.

Let's make the distinction visible.

<Interactive kind="dependence" preset="students" id="student-counterexample" />

At first, Maria solved Problem 1, and Noah solved Problem 2.

The teacher's statement is true. Notice that no single problem was solved by both students, yet every student solved at least one problem.

Now introduce a third student, Lea, who hasn't solved any of the problems.

Suddenly, the original statement is false. Not because *everyone* failed, but because one particular student fails the condition completely.

This is the fundamental logic of negation.

To negate a universal claim, we only need one counterexample.

And to negate the existential claim within it, we need to show that none of the possible choices works.

Let's translate this reasoning into symbols.

We begin with the original statement:

$$
 \forall s\;\exists p\;S(s,p).
$$

Its negation is

$$
 \neg(\forall s\;\exists p\;S(s,p)).
$$

The symbol $\neg$ means "it is not the case that."

First, saying "not every student" means there exists a student for whom the remaining claim fails:

$$
 \exists s\;\neg(\exists p\;S(s,p)).
$$

But saying "there does not exist a problem that this student solved" means that every problem is one they did not solve:

$$
 \boxed{\exists s\;\forall p\;\neg S(s,p)}
$$

That's the final negation.

<ReasoningFigure kind="quantifier-negation" />

More generally, we have two important equivalences:

$$
 \neg\forall x\\,P(x)\equiv\exists x\\,\neg P(x)
$$

$$
 \neg\exists x\\,P(x)\equiv\forall x\\,\neg P(x).
$$

Notice that the second equivalence says that "there is no $x$ for which $P(x)$ is true" means that $P(x)$ is false for every $x$.

And the first says that "not every $x$ satisfies $P$" means that at least one $x$ fails.

These aren't arbitrary rules for manipulating symbols. They are exactly what our counterexample investigation has already shown.

## 9. Does "there exists" mean exactly one?

Before we conclude, there's one more distinction we need to make.

Suppose I tell you:

"There exists a person Alice knows."

You might imagine one particular acquaintance. But mathematically, the statement only requires at least one.

It doesn't require exactly one.

Let's look at three possible situations.

<ReasoningFigure kind="existence-counts" />

If Alice knows nobody, the existential statement is false.

If Alice knows Ben, it's true.

And if Alice knows both Ben and Clara, it's still true.

The mathematical symbol $\exists$ requires **one or more witnesses**, not exactly one.

When mathematicians want to say that exactly one object satisfies a condition, they express *unique existence*, often written with the symbol $\exists!$. We'll investigate that distinction more thoroughly in Episode 21.

For now, remember that finding a second witness doesn't invalidate an existential statement. It simply means we have more than one possible way to satisfy it.

## 10. Can you build a counterexample yourself?

We've seen how to read statements with two quantifiers, how reversing their order can change their meaning, and how negation works when quantifiers are nested.

Now let's see whether you can use those ideas without relying on our original network.

Imagine a library with several books and several sources. Define

$$
 C(b,s)
$$

to mean that book $b$ cites source $s$.

Consider the following statement:

$$
 \forall b\;\exists s\;C(b,s).
$$

Try translating it into ordinary language.

It means every book cites at least one source.

Now change the quantifier order.

$$
 \exists s\;\forall b\;C(b,s).
$$

What does this mean?

It means there is one source that every book cites.

Can you imagine a library where the first statement is true, but the second one is false?

<Exercise>

<ReasoningFigure kind="citation-witnesses" />

Here is one possible answer. The first book cites Source 1. The second cites Source 2. And the third cites Source 3.

Every book has a citation, so the first statement is true.

But there is no source shared by all three books, so the second statement is false.

It's the same logical structure as Alice, Ben, and Clara, only now we've applied it to a different situation.

</Exercise>

And one final question:

What would it mean for the first statement to be false?

Not every book cites a source.

Which means that there must be **at least one book that cites no source at all**.

In symbols,

$$
 \boxed{\exists b\;\forall s\;\neg C(b,s)}
$$

If you can explain that translation, and understand why our little network separates the first two statements, then you've grasped the central idea of this episode.

## 11. What we've really discovered

At the beginning, we asked whether everyone knowing someone meant that someone must be known by everyone.

The answer was no.

But the deeper discovery wasn't really about knowing people.

It was about the order of our choices.

When we write

$$
 \forall x\;\exists y,
$$

we're allowed to choose a suitable $y$ separately for every $x$.

But when we write

$$
 \exists y\;\forall x,
$$

we must find one $y$ that works for every $x$.

That's why the order of the quantifiers matters.

It's also why negating a statement like "everyone has at least one" gives us "someone has none," rather than "everyone has none."

And it's why a single counterexample can be enough to disprove a universal statement.

In ordinary conversation, the difference between these sentences can be easy to overlook. But in mathematics, even a tiny change in their structure can mean the difference between a true statement and a false one.

The next time you encounter a claim involving words like *every*, *some*, *at least one*, or *none*, try asking yourself:

**Who gets to choose, and does that choice have to work for everyone?**

Sometimes, understanding a statement begins with nothing more than noticing the order in which its questions are asked.
