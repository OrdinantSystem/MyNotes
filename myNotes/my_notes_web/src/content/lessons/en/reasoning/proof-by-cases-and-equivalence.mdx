## 1. A pattern that nobody seems able to escape

Imagine that I give you a very simple challenge.

Choose any integer you want, multiply it by the integer immediately after it, and try to obtain an odd number.

You can choose a positive number, a negative number, or zero. There are no restrictions beyond choosing an integer.

Let's begin with something simple.

<Interactive kind="cases" preset="consecutive" id="cases-consecutive" />

Four multiplied by five gives twenty. Seven multiplied by eight gives fifty-six. Even if we move into negative numbers, something similar happens.

$$
\begin{aligned} 4\cdot5&=20\\ 7\cdot8&=56\\ (-3)\cdot(-2)&=6\\ 0\cdot1&=0 \end{aligned}
$$

Every result is even.

But as we discovered in earlier episodes, finding a pattern and proving that a pattern can never fail are two fundamentally different achievements.

Our conjecture is not merely that this happens frequently. We are claiming that it happens for **every possible integer**.

$$
\boxed{\forall n\in\mathbb Z,\quad 2\mid n(n+1)}
$$

The notation $2\mid n(n+1)$ means that two divides the product without leaving a remainder.

There are infinitely many integers. We cannot test them all individually.

But perhaps we don't need to.

Perhaps instead of investigating infinitely many numbers, we can investigate the different *kinds* of numbers that could appear.

## 2. Reducing infinitely many possibilities to two

Let's return to our number line, but this time, instead of concentrating on the actual values of the numbers, let's concentrate on a single property.

Their parity.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-parity" />

The particular integer we choose can change. Its magnitude can change. Its sign can change.

But regardless of those differences, it must belong to one of these two categories.

Now let's look at what happens when we select two consecutive integers.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-possibilities" />

If the first integer is even, the second is odd.

If the first integer is odd, the second is even.

We can never select two consecutive integers that are both odd.

And this suggests something interesting. Perhaps the reason the product is even changes depending on the integer we choose.

Sometimes the first factor provides the factor of two. Sometimes the second factor provides it.

The conclusion remains the same, but the route by which we reach it changes.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-routes" />

This is the fundamental idea behind **proof by cases**.

Instead of searching for one argument that handles all possibilities simultaneously, we divide the possibilities into groups and construct an argument for each group.

But before this becomes a mathematical proof, we need to establish two things.

First, that our groups cover every possible integer. Second, that our conclusion genuinely follows within each group.

## 3. Turning the two routes into a mathematical proof

In the previous episode, we learned that understanding the shape of our goal helps us discover what our next step should be.

Let's bring back that same mathematical workspace.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-workspace" />

Our goal is to find a factor of two somewhere inside the product.

But the only information we initially have is that $n$ is an integer.

How can we make that information more useful?

Every integer can be expressed in exactly one of these two forms:

$$
n=2k \qquad\text{or}\qquad n=2k+1,
$$

where $k\in\mathbb Z$.

This follows from integer division: dividing an integer by two always leaves a remainder of zero or one.

So our two cases are not arbitrary guesses. They are a complete description of the possible remainders.

### Case 1 — Suppose $n$ is even

Let's begin with the left branch.

We can now introduce some information that wasn't available before:

$$
n=2k,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$

Our two consecutive integers therefore look like this:

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-even-pairs" />

$$
n(n+1)=(2k)(2k+1)
$$

And because the first factor already contains a two, we can move that two outside the expression.

$$
\begin{aligned} n(n+1) &=(2k)(2k+1)\\[4pt] &=\underbrace{2}_{\text{factor of two}} \underbrace{\bigl[k(2k+1)\bigr]}_{\text{an integer}}. \end{aligned}
$$

Now compare this expression with our original goal.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-even-match" />

By choosing $m=k(2k+1)$, we have reached the required form. Both $k$ and $2k+1$ are integers, so $m$ is an integer.

We have proved that if $n$ is even, its product with the next integer is even.

But we must be careful here.

We haven't proved our original universal claim yet. We have proved only one branch of it.

### Case 2 — Suppose $n$ is odd

Now we return to the point where we divided the argument.

This time,

$$
n=2k+1,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$

The first factor is no longer immediately useful. But observe what happens when we increase it by one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-odd-successor" />

$$
\underbrace{2k+1}_{\text{odd}}+1 = \underbrace{2(k+1)}_{\text{even}}
$$

The previously unpaired object has acquired a partner.

Algebraically, we can express that transformation as

$$
n+1=2k+2=2(k+1).
$$

So even though the first factor doesn't provide the factor of two we need, the second factor does.

$$
\begin{aligned} n(n+1) &=(2k+1)\,2(k+1)\\[4pt] &=\underbrace{2}_{\text{factor of two}} \underbrace{\bigl[(2k+1)(k+1)\bigr]}_{\text{an integer}}. \end{aligned}
$$

And again, we have reached our target form.

$$
n(n+1)=2m,
$$

this time with

$$
m=(2k+1)(k+1).
$$

Since $2k+1$ and $k+1$ are integers, this witness $m$ is an integer.

Look at what has happened to our original workspace.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-completed" />

The integer expressions inside the brackets are different. But both expressions satisfy precisely the same definition of an even number.

And because there is no integer outside our two cases, there is no integer for which our argument fails to establish the conclusion.

We have taken a problem involving infinitely many objects and reduced it to two mathematical arguments.

That is the power of proof by cases.

## 4. When correct arguments produce an incomplete proof

Now imagine that another mathematician approaches the same problem differently.

Instead of dividing the integers into even and odd, they decide to divide them into positive and negative integers.

Suppose they successfully prove our claim for every positive integer. They then successfully prove it for every negative integer.

Both arguments are mathematically correct.

Have they proved our original claim?

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-missing-zero" />

There is one integer that belongs to neither of those categories.

Zero.

And although we already know that our conclusion happens to hold for zero, the proposed proof hasn't accounted for it.

This is a particularly interesting kind of mathematical mistake. Neither of the individual arguments needs to contain an error. The failure exists in the relationship between the arguments and the original claim.

The branches are correct, but the structure is incomplete.

We say that a collection of cases is **exhaustive** when every relevant possibility belongs to at least one of the cases.

But does every possibility need to belong to *exactly* one case?

Let's compare three different ways of dividing a mathematical world.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-coverage" />

The first representation is problematic because some possibilities remain outside our argument.

The second representation works perfectly. Every possible object belongs to precisely one case.

But the third representation also works.

For example, we could divide the integers into those satisfying

$$
n\leq 0
$$

and those satisfying

$$
n\geq 0.
$$

Zero belongs to both groups. But that doesn't invalidate the argument. If we prove our conclusion in both groups, zero is simply covered twice.

Overlapping cases may introduce redundant work, but redundancy is not a logical error.

For a proof by cases, what matters is that nothing is left uncovered.

### The underlying logical structure

Remember our discussion of OR and implication in previous episodes.

Suppose we have two possible conditions, $P$ and $Q$, and we know that at least one of them must hold.

$$
P\lor Q
$$

We then establish that the first condition implies our desired conclusion $R$.

$$
P\Rightarrow R
$$

And independently, we establish that the second condition also implies $R$.

$$
Q\Rightarrow R
$$

Regardless of which possibility holds, $R$ follows.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-logic" />

And now we can express the entire reasoning structure as one logical principle:

$$
\boxed{ \bigl[(P\lor Q) \land(P\Rightarrow R) \land(Q\Rightarrow R)\bigr] \Rightarrow R }
$$

This is what our original proof was doing beneath all the algebra.

Even and odd integers were simply one particular application of a much more general logical structure.

## 5. Two arrows that mean something entirely different

Now I want to introduce another mathematical problem that also seems to require two arguments.

Consider the following claim:

**An integer is even if and only if its square is even.**

Let's begin by investigating it visually.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-square-samples" />

Even numbers seem to produce even squares. Odd numbers seem to produce odd squares.

But look carefully at the language of our claim.

We didn't simply say that **if** an integer is even, its square is even.

We said that an integer is even **if and only if** its square is even.

What additional obligation does that introduce?

Let's name our two statements.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-properties" />

If we establish that every even integer has an even square, we obtain the following implication.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-forward" />

But suppose I give you an integer without telling you whether it is even or odd. I tell you only that its square is even.

Can you conclude that the original integer is even?

That's a different question.

We are now beginning with $Q$ and trying to reach $P$.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-reverse" />

This is what we mean by logical equivalence.

$$
\boxed{ P\Leftrightarrow Q \quad\equiv\quad (P\Rightarrow Q)\land(Q\Rightarrow P) }
$$

Both implications must hold.

And although this also requires two arguments, notice the difference from our previous proof.

In proof by cases, we begin with different possibilities and try to reach the same conclusion.

Here, we begin with one statement, prove the other, and then exchange their roles.

We are not dividing the domain into two cases. We are establishing a relationship between two mathematical properties.

## 6. Why the second direction requires a different idea

Let's try to prove our equivalence, beginning with the first direction.

### Direction 1 — If an integer is even, its square is even

Our workspace is relatively straightforward.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-square-workspace" />

Suppose we represent a positive even integer as a length, and then use that length to construct a square.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-even-square" />

$$
(2k)^2=\underbrace{2k^2+2k^2}_{\text{Two equal integer parts}}
$$

The square can be separated into two equal rectangles. Each rectangle contains an integer number of unit squares.

And that is exactly what our algebra establishes.

$$
\begin{aligned} n^2&=(2k)^2\\ &=4k^2\\ &=2(2k^2). \end{aligned}
$$

Since $2k^2$ is an integer, $n^2$ must be even.

The diagram helped us visualize the factor of two, while the algebra establishes the result for every even integer, including negative integers and zero.

Our first implication is proved.

$$
\boxed{n\text{ even}\Rightarrow n^2\text{ even}}
$$

### Direction 2 — If the square is even, the integer is even

Now let's reverse the question.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-reverse-workspace" />

At first, we might be tempted to reverse our previous calculation.

If

$$
n^2=2m,
$$

then perhaps

$$
n=\pm\sqrt{2m}.
$$

But does this expression reveal that $n$ is even?

Not immediately.

We would still need to establish why this integer must contain a factor of two.

Our previous method doesn't automatically provide the answer. We need a new route.

So let's return to what we learned at the beginning of this episode.

Every integer is either even or odd.

If we know that the square is even, can we eliminate one of those possibilities?

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-eliminate-before" />

What happens when we square an odd number?

Let's investigate geometrically before doing the calculation.

<Interactive kind="cases" preset="odd-square" id="cases-odd-square" />

We can separate the odd square into four regions. The area model uses positive side lengths; the algebra below applies to every integer witness $k$, including negative values.

There is a central square whose side length is $2k$. There are two additional strips, each containing $2k$ unit squares. And finally, there is one corner square left over.

The central square contains an even number of objects. Both additional strips contain an even number of objects.

But there is always one additional object.

And that single object changes the parity of the entire square.

Let's translate the construction into algebra.

$$
\begin{aligned} n^2&=(2k+1)^2\\[4pt] &=(2k)(2k)+(2k)(1)\\ &\phantom{=}+(1)(2k)+(1)(1)\\[4pt] &=4k^2+4k+1. \end{aligned}
$$

The first two terms share a factor of two.

$$
n^2= \underbrace{2(2k^2+2k)}_{\text{Even}} +\underbrace{1}_{\text{Extra unit}}.
$$

This is exactly the form of an odd integer.

Therefore,

$$
n\text{ odd}\Rightarrow n^2\text{ odd}.
$$

Now remember what we were trying to prove.

We were given an integer whose square is even.

But we have just established that if the integer were odd, its square would necessarily be odd.

So that possibility is incompatible with our assumption.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-eliminate-after" />

Because every integer is either even or odd, and because an odd integer cannot have an even square, the integer must be even.

We have established the reverse direction.

$$
\boxed{n^2\text{ even}\Rightarrow n\text{ even}}
$$

This particular strategy is called **proof by contraposition**, which we will investigate more deeply in the next episode.

For now, what matters is that we have found a route that works.

The first direction was established directly by manipulating the definition of an even integer.

The second direction was established by investigating what happens when the desired conclusion fails.

Two different mathematical strategies, establishing two different implications.

$$
\boxed{ n\text{ is even} \iff n^2\text{ is even} }
$$

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-equivalence" />

Only now have we proved the equivalence.

## 7. Can a true theorem have an invalid proof?

Imagine that you open a mathematics textbook and encounter the following argument.

<Note title="Proposed proof — to inspect">

**Claim:** An integer is even if and only if its square is even.

Suppose $n$ is even. Then there exists an integer $k$ such that

$$
n=2k.
$$

Consequently,

$$
n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2).
$$

Since $2k^2$ is an integer, $n^2$ is even.

Therefore,

$$
n\text{ even}\iff n^2\text{ even}.
$$

$\square$

</Note>

Everything in the calculation is correct.

The conclusion is also true. We have just proved it ourselves.

So is this a valid proof?

<Interactive kind="cases" preset="inspection" id="cases-inspection" />

This is another example of something we discovered earlier in the course: a person can arrive at the correct conclusion without having supplied a sufficient justification for it.

The problem is not that their conclusion is false.

The problem is that their conclusion is stronger than what their argument establishes.

Their actual achievement is

$$
P\Rightarrow Q.
$$

But their announced achievement is

$$
P\Leftrightarrow Q.
$$

These are not interchangeable.

To inspect a mathematical proof, we must compare what the statement requires with what the argument actually delivers.

Sometimes the difference is an entire missing branch. Sometimes it is a single missing implication.

## 8. What if there are more than two possibilities?

Until now, our proof by cases has involved two possibilities.

But there is nothing mathematically special about the number two.

Let's modify our original problem.

Instead of multiplying two consecutive integers, we will multiply three.

$$
n(n+1)(n+2)
$$

And instead of asking whether the result is divisible by two, we will ask whether it is always divisible by three.

$$
\boxed{ \forall n\in\mathbb Z,\quad 3\mid n(n+1)(n+2) }
$$

Let's look at our number line again. But this time, instead of coloring integers according to their parity, we will color them according to their remainder when divided by three.

<Interactive kind="cases" preset="triples" id="cases-triples" />

Every integer has a remainder of zero, one, or two when divided by three.

Which means that every integer belongs to one of these three categories:

$$
\begin{aligned} n&=3k,\\ n&=3k+1,\\ n&=3k+2. \end{aligned}
$$

Now, before we continue, try to predict which factor will be divisible by three in each case.

<Interactive kind="cases" preset="factors" id="cases-factors" />

If $n=3k$, then the first factor is already divisible by three.

If $n=3k+1$, then the third factor becomes

$$
n+2=3k+3=3(k+1).
$$

And if $n=3k+2$, then the second factor becomes

$$
n+1=3k+3=3(k+1).
$$

We can now construct a proof with three branches instead of two.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-three-branches" />

In every possible case, we have identified a factor divisible by three. Consequently, the entire product is divisible by three.

And because the three cases exhaust all possible integer remainders, our proof is complete.

But perhaps you can already see another pattern emerging.

What would happen if we multiplied four consecutive integers? Or five? Or a hundred?

Could we use the same reasoning to prove that the product of any $m$ consecutive integers is divisible by $m$, for every positive integer $m$?

Think about what would happen to the number of possible remainders.

$$
\underbrace{0,1,2,\ldots,m-1}_{m\text{ possible remainders}}
$$

We have not yet written that general proof. But we have discovered the structure from which such a proof could emerge.

And that is often how mathematical progress works. A method that originally solves one problem becomes a way of understanding an entire family of problems.

## 9. Understanding the architecture of an argument

Let's return to the two mathematical structures we have investigated today.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-architecture" />

In the first structure, our challenge is to account for every possibility.

In the second structure, our challenge is to establish every required direction.

Both require us to recognize that a mathematical claim may contain several obligations, even when it is expressed in a single sentence.

And this changes how we approach proofs.

Instead of immediately asking ourselves, "How do I prove this?", we can begin by asking something more fundamental.

"What exactly would need to be established for this statement to be true?"

Sometimes the answer is one calculation. Sometimes it is several cases. Sometimes it is two implications requiring entirely different methods.

Learning mathematics is not simply learning how to travel from one statement to another.

It is also learning to recognize the architecture of the journey before we begin.

Because a proof is not complete when we have found one convincing route.

**A proof is complete when every route required by the claim has been justified.**

### Exercise 1 — Inspecting coverage

Determine which collections of cases exhaust the set of all integers.

<Interactive kind="cases" preset="coverage-practice" id="cases-coverage-practice" />

### Exercise 2 — Constructing a three-case proof

Prove that the square of every integer leaves a remainder of either zero or one when divided by three.

$$
\forall n\in\mathbb Z,\quad n^2\bmod 3\in\{0,1\}.
$$

YOUR PROOF WORKSPACE

| Case         | Square         | Remainder |
| ------------ | -------------- | --------- |
| $n=3k$   | $(3k)^2$   | ?         |
| $n=3k+1$ | $(3k+1)^2$ | ?         |
| $n=3k+2$ | $(3k+2)^2$ | ?         |

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** Write $n=3k+r$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$ and $r\in\{0,1,2\}$.

| Case | Square | Remainder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $n=3k$ | $n^2=3(3k^2)$ | $0$ |
| $n=3k+1$ | $n^2=3(3k^2+2k)+1$ | $1$ |
| $n=3k+2$ | $n^2=3(3k^2+4k+1)+1$ | $1$ |

Each parenthesized expression is an integer. The three remainders exhaust all integers, so a square never has remainder $2$ modulo $3$.
</Exercise>

### Exercise 3 — An equivalence that fails

Consider the following claim:

$$
6\mid n\iff 3\mid n.
$$

Determine which direction is true and which direction is false. Then provide a proof of the true implication and a counterexample to the false implication.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-divisibility" />

<Exercise>

Forward implication: true.

$$
n=6k=3(2k)
$$

Every multiple of six is a multiple of three.

Reverse implication: false.

Choose $n=9$.

$$
3\mid9,\qquad 6\nmid9.
$$

Therefore, the proposed equivalence is false.

</Exercise>

### Exercise 4 — Choosing your own cases

Prove the following statement for every real number $x$:

$$
\boxed{|x|\geq x}
$$

Unlike our previous examples, this problem concerns real numbers rather than integers. Parity is therefore not an appropriate way to divide the domain.

Find two cases based on the definition of absolute value.

<ReasoningFigure kind="cases-absolute" />

<Exercise>

By definition,

$$
|x|= \begin{cases} x,&x\geq0,\\ -x,&x<0. \end{cases}
$$

If $x\geq0$, then $|x|=x$, so the inequality holds.

If $x<0$, then $-x>0>x$. Consequently, $|x|=-x>x$.

The cases exhaust all real numbers. Therefore,

$$
|x|\geq x
$$

for every $x\in\mathbb R$. $\square$

</Exercise>

### Exercise 5 — Generalization challenge

Let $m$ be a positive integer. Prove that the product of any $m$ consecutive integers is divisible by $m$.

$$
\boxed{ m\mid\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(n+j) }
$$

This exercise generalizes the central investigation of the episode.

The challenge is to avoid writing a separate proof for each possible value of $m$, or manually describing every possible remainder. Instead, find a way to reason about an arbitrary remainder $r$.

<Exercise>
**Reference solution (added).** Let $m\in\mathbb Z$ with $m\geq1$ and take any $n\in\mathbb Z$. By Euclidean division, write $n=qm+r$ with $q\in\mathbb Z$ and $0\leq r<m$.

If $r=0$, choose $j=0$; then $n+j=qm$. If $r>0$, choose $j=m-r$. Here $1\leq j\leq m-1$ and $n+j=(q+1)m$. Thus one of the listed factors is a multiple of $m$.

All other factors are integers, so the entire product is a multiple of $m$. For $m=1$, the product has the single factor $n$, which is divisible by $1$. This also handles zero and negative starting integers.
</Exercise>
